\title{Fertility, Household Wealth, and Maternal Nutrition among Indian Social Groups} 
\date{\today}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Maternal undernutrition is a threat to women’s health and a leading cause of child mortality and morbidity in India. This paper uses nationally representative data to estimate the fraction of women who begin pregnancy underweight -- a key indicator of maternal nutrition -- and presents the first estimates by caste and religion.  We use nonparametric reweighting to show that more than one in five Indian women start pregnancy underweight.  Adivasis and Dalits are 55\%, and 40\%, more likely, respectively, to be underweight before pregnancy than Forward Caste Hindus and Muslims. Fertility and birth spacing, which are often assumed to predict maternal nutrition, do not explain the gaps we observe.  Instead, differences in household wealth explain a large fraction of the gaps. Notably, and despite multidimensional disadvantage, a similar fraction of Muslim and Forward Caste Hindu women start pregnancy underweight.  A maternal nutrition advantage among Muslim women appears when we account for the fact that Muslim women live in poorer households. 
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Maternal nutrition is a critical determinant of the health and survival of both a mother and her child. During pregnancy, a energy requirements rise substantially to support fetal growth and safe delivery. When women begin pregnancy undernourished — with limited fat and muscle stores, micronutrient deficiencies, and reduced immune and circulatory capacity — they are less able to meet these demands. The inter-generational transmission of health is perhaps nowhere more evident than it is during pregnancy. Nutrition is an important pathway: undernourished mothers are more likely to have low birth weight and preterm babies, who face a higher risk of death than babies born to better nourished mothers \citep{ahmed2013global, aizer2014intergenerational}.  

Prior research has documented a disproportionately high burden of maternal undernutrition in India relative to the country’s level of economic development.  India is the country with the largest absolute number of undernourished mothers \citep{bhutta2000has, coffey2015prepregnancy}. Maternal undernutrition causes low birth weight and prematurity among infants.  These together constitute the leading cause of child mortality in India \citep{fadel2017changes}. Among children who survive infancy, those born to undernourished mothers are more likely to grow up to be malnourished and to suffer from chronic illnesses due to the lasting consequences of poor prenatal health \citep{underwood2002health, almond2011killing}.

One population-level indicator of maternal nutrition is the fraction of women who begin pregnancy underweight. The prepregnancy period is critical for nutrition; \cite{stephenson2018before} recommend focusing interventions before conception to improve maternal and child outcomes. Health guidelines also recommend that women who have a prepregnancy body mass index (BMI) of less than 18.5 -- the World Health Organization's cutoff for chronic energy deficiency \citep{who_nlis_malnutrition_women_2021} -- gain 28 to 40 pounds during pregnancy to compensate for poor reserves \citep{national2009weight, ismail2016gestational}.  However, women in low-income settings tend to gain little weight during pregnancy, leaving them and their children at risk of adverse outcomes \citep{coffey2015prepregnancy, wang2020gestational, perumal2023suboptimal}.

National pregnancy health monitoring systems, such as the birth registration data from the United States Center for Health Statistics, the Danish Medical Birth Registry, and the Birth Registry of Norway, collect self-reported height and prepregnancy weight -- ``[what was] your weight immediately before you became pregnancy with this child?'' -- from women who have recently given birth \citep{cornish2024maternal}. These data allow researchers to track prepregnancy nutrition and study its relationship with maternal and birth outcomes. In contrast, no comparable, representative longitudinal monitoring system exists in India. However, the large-sample, representative, cross-sectional Demographic and Health Surveys, which measure women's heights and weights, allow researchers to estimate the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight by applying statistical adjustments to the weights of nonpregnant women. Simply estimating the prevalence of underweight among nonpregnant women, without statistical adjustment, could produce a biased estimate of prepregnancy underweight.  Not all nonpregnant women are prepregnant; many may not be planning to conceive again or be highly unlikely to become pregnant.  Women who are unlikely to conceive may have systematically different nutritional status than those who are actually about to conceive. The bias generated by equating nonpregnant status with prepregnant status may be especially important in India, where social hierarchies based on age and sex mean that women in their childbearing years are particularly vulnerable to undernutrition \citep{gupta1995life, coffey2021neonatal, coffey2022mothers}.

To compute a more accurate estimate of prepregnancy underweight from cross-sectional data, \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy} constructs a nonparametric reweighting function to correct for selection into pregnancy.  That research finds that in 2005, 42\% of Indian women began pregnancy underweight.  This rate was nearly two and half times what it was among sub-Saharan African women in the same period. This paper uses the same method to estimate the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight in India in 2019-21.  We find that 22\% of women begin pregnancy with a BMI below 18.5. In comparison, \cite{miller2025preconception} estimates a prevalence of preconception malnutrition of 14\% for India in 2019-21 without adjusting for selection into pregnancy; our work demonstrates that this is an underestimate.

This paper also contributes the first estimates of prepregnancy underweight stratified by Indian social group. It joins a growing literature on health disparities among caste and religious groups in India, where national averages mask stark group-level inequalities \citep{vyas2022social, gupta2020caste, coffey2019local, lopalo2019consequences, geruso2018neighborhood, bora2019persistent}. We find that the burden of prepregnancy underweight is highest among Adivasi, or indigenous, women (28\%), followed by Dalits, a group of oppressed castes who have been treated as ``untouchable'' (25\%).  Muslim women who are not from a Dalit or Adivasi background have similar rates of prepregnancy underweight as Hindu women from dominant (``Forward'') castes (17\% and 18\%, respectively).  

Beyond documenting disparities, this study also investigates explanations. We first consider whether differences in fertility and birth spacing can account for social group differences in prepregnancy underweight.  We test this hypothesis because prior research on maternal and infant health emphasizes the role of high parity and short birth spacing in ``depleting'' mothers' nutritional status \citep{winkvist1992new}.  Using a Kitagawa decomposition, we find that differences in parity and birth spacing between Forward and oppressed caste women account for very little of the gaps that we document.  

Second, we consider whether differences in household wealth can explain the gaps in prepregnancy underweight that we observe.  Using a Kitagawa decomposition, we find that the distributions of women into wealth quartiles explains about 70\% of the Forward caste-Adivasi gap, and about 65\% of the Forward caste-Dalit gap in prepregnancy underweight. Although the difference between Forward caste and Muslim prepregnancy underweight was not statistically significant, accounting for wealth differences reveals that Muslim women are less likely to be underweight at the start of pregnancy than Forward caste women. This is consistent with research on health disparities that documents health advantages among Indian Muslims relative to Hindus, despite socioeconomic disadvantage \citep{guillot2010hindu, bhalotra2010puzzle, visaria2015preference, geruso2018neighborhood, sharma_gupta_2025}.  We note that although differences in the distribution of women into wealth categories can explain a majority of the gaps we observe, large fractions of the gaps remain unexplained, implying the presence of other factors in determining prepregnancy underweight and disparities in maternal nutrition.

In the next section, we present background information on maternal and child health, on social groups, and on population health in India.  We review the literature on the causes of maternal undernutrition and then describe our data source.  We use the fifth round of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS) (2019-21), India's Demographic and Health Survey.  We then describe the two complementary decomposition methods that we use to analyze maternal nutrition.  The first is a nonparametric reweighting technique that corrects for selection into pregnancy.  The second is a Kitagawa decomposition that helps us investigate candidate explanations for group-level disparities in prepregnancy underweight.  We present results on the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight for the population as a whole, and by social group.  We then calculate what percent of the gaps in prepregnancy underweight between Forward caste Hindu women and women from marginalized groups can be explained by differences in parity and birth spacing, on the one hand, and differences in household wealth, on the other.  Finally, we discuss our results and consider implications for policy and future research.

\section{Background}

\subsection{Indian social groups and population health}

Caste and religion are important axes of social inequality in India \citep{vaid2014caste, kumar2016explaining, varshney2002ethnic, majid2015indian}. A person's \textit{jati}, or caste sub-group, influences their social interactions, marriage, occupation, and life chances \citep{deshpande2011grammar, thorat2012blocked}. Although caste is a concept closely associated with Hinduism, people from all religious groups in South Asia are stratified along caste lines. 

In an effort to address historical and ongoing discrimination, the central and state governments of India have affirmative action for people from marginalized groups in higher education and government jobs. Affirmative action policies were written into the Indian Constitution, which recognizes three historically disadvantaged sets of \textit{jatis}. 

Scheduled Castes (SC), comprising about 20\% of India's population \citep{iips_icf_nfhs5_2022}, are \textit{jatis} that were traditionally considered ``untouchable.''  Both historically and today, Scheduled Castes do stigmatized work, such as work that involves contact with dead bodies (leather work, cremation, removal of dead animals) or feces (cleaning latrines and bathrooms, maintaining sewers, emptying latrine pits).  People from Scheduled Castes refer to themselves as \textit{Dalit}, or ``oppressed people.''  Other Backward Classes (OBC) comprise about 43\% of India's population \citep{iips_icf_nfhs5_2022} are are from \textit{jatis} considered socially, economically, or educationally deprived compared to the dominant, or ``Forward,'' castes.  ``Backward'' is a term used in the Constitution; we use the abbreviation ``OBC'' with no normative implications. Scheduled Tribes (ST), comprising 9\% of the total population \citep{iips_icf_nfhs5_2022}, refer to India’s indigenous groups who face persistent disadvantage due to geographic isolation and exclusion from state services.  To refer to Scheduled Tribes, we use the term ``Adivasi,'' a term of self-identification that means ``early inhabitants.''  In referring to socially dominant Hindu \textit{jatis}, we use the term, ``Forward caste,'' which is used in the NFHS answer options.

Muslims, India's largest religious minority, are about 16\% of the population \citep{iips_icf_nfhs5_2022}.  Historically and today, Muslims face social exclusion and targeted violence.  Political movements linking Indian nationalism with Hinduism are central to the marginalization of Muslims \citep{farooqui2020political}. Muslims belong to Dalit, Adivasi, OBC, and Forward caste jatis \citep{ahmad2007recognition}, but Muslims are typically not eligible for affirmative action \citep{hasan2018muslims, hasan2022wider}.     

The inequalities created by caste and religion shape population health in India. Dalits, Adivasis, and Muslims have lower life expectancies than Forward caste Hindus \citep{vyas2022social, gupta2022large}.  Life expectancy gaps between social groups are comparable to those seen by race, ethnicity, or religion in the United States and South Africa. \cite{gupta2020caste} find more self-reported depression and anxiety among Dalits and Adivasis than among Forward caste Hindus.  \cite{coffey2019local} document gaps in child height among SC, ST, and OBC children when compared to children from Forward castes. Caste disparities are only partially explained by differences in socioeconomic status \citep{bora2019persistent, kumar2016explaining, van2009decomposing, vyas2022social, dutta2020effect}. 

Social marginalization does not always correspond with health disadvantage.  Research finds better health for Muslim children and women on some indicators.  \cite{geruso2018neighborhood} show that Muslim infants are more likely to survive because they are exposed to better neighborhood sanitation, on average, than Hindu infants. \cite{sharma_gupta_2025} find that Muslim women are less likely to experience domestic violence than Hindu women, and that the difference can largely be explained by lower use of alcohol among Muslim men.  \cite{visaria2015preference} finds that selective abortion of girls is lower among Muslims than Hindus.

\subsection{Maternal and child nutrition in India}

In the late 1990s, researchers noticed an ``Asian Engima:'' Asian children had higher rates of malnutrition than children in sub-Saharan Africa, despite being less poor, on average \citep{ramalingaswami1996commentary, underwood2002health}.  They posited that maternal malnutrition, related to the low social status of Indian women, contributed to a high prevalence of low birthweight and widespread child malnurition.  Using the third round of the NFHS (2005), \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy} found that 42\% of Indian women were underweight before pregnancy, and that the average Indian woman gained so little weight during pregnancy that she weighed less at the end of pregnancy than the average woman in sub-Saharan Africa weighed at the beginning of pregnancy.  Research has established links between the social status of women within Indian households and their nutrition \citep{coffey2022mothers, hathi2021women}.  

\subsection{Fertility, birth spacing, and nutrition among Indian social groups}

Research on maternal health in low and middle income countries studies ``maternal depletion,'' the hypothesis that higher-order births and low spacing between births leave women nutritionally vulnerable \citep{winkvist1992new, king2003risk, shell2004maternal, bauserman2020relationship}.  Can differences in fertility and birth spacing explain differences in prepregnancy underweight across Indian social groups? 

Higher fertility among Muslims compared to Hindus has been widely studied \citep{dharmalingam2004pervasive, bhat2005role, james2005accelerated} and has featured prominently in political discourse \citep{jeffery2002population, singh2020numbering}.  Although less well studied, each round of the NFHS finds higher fertility rates among Advisais, Dalits, and OBCs than among Forward castes. \cite{srinivas2024rapid} find that fertility differences across castes are decreasing over time. The literature also notes differences in birth spacing across caste and religious groups, with Forward caste women being most likely to have two or more years between births \citep{kundu2025short, maity2017comparing, bhalotra2008birth, singh2024wealth}.  

Whether differences in fertility and birth spacing will explain differences in prepregnancy underweight will depend on: 1) the magnitude of fertility and birth spacing differences across groups, and 2) the relationship between these variables and maternal nutrition. \cite{coffey2021neonatal} provide suggestive evidence that, in India, higher parity may \emph{not} lead to maternal depletion.  They find that, controlling for siblingship size, higher-parity births are more likely to survive the first month of life than lower-parity births.  They posit that Indian women's social status, and consequently the balance of their food intake and work output, may be linked to age and parity progression, allowing them to gain weight across successive pregnancies. 

\subsection{Household wealth and nutrition among Indian social groups}

\cite{tagade2020intergroup} estimate that, in 2013, two-thirds of India's wealth was controlled by Hindu Forward caste and OBC groups. Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Muslims each had 10\%, 6\%, and 9\% of India's total wealth, respectively.  These estimated wealth shares were considerably less than their 2013 population shares of 18\%, 9\%, and 12\%.  

There are several pathways through which household wealth may protect against undernutrition: it allows people to buy more and higher quality food \citep{raghunathan2021affordability}, they are more likely to have tools and technology that reduce their energy output \citep{deaton2009food}, and they may be able to afford cleaner water and better sanitation, which reduces energy loss to disease \citep{bhutta2008works}.  Since marginalized castes are more likely to be poor, and to remain poor over time \citep{thorat2012has, thorat2017escaping}, we expect differences in household wealth to explain at least part of the gaps in prepregnancy underweight across  social groups. 

We note that the relationship between household wealth and women's nutrition is not straightForward.  Calorie consumption in India declined between the 1980s and early 2000s, despite economic growth and reductions in poverty,  and despite the fact that Indians were significantly more likely to be underweight than people in other low- and middle-income countries \citep{deaton2009food}.  Data from the 1980s found that, more so than the nutrient intake of men in the same household, women's nutrient intake declined when food prices rose \citep{behrman1990intrahousehold}.  In the 2011 Indian Human Development Survey (IHDS), a quarter of Indian women reported that they are expected to eat after the men in their households \citep{hathi2021women}.  

Even if increases in wealth are less likely to translate into improvements in the body mass of young women in India than of young women in settings with less gendered foodways, cross-household differences in wealth may nevertheless partially explain cross-group differences in prepregnancy body mass if women in different social groups have similar access to their households' food or rest budgets, or if overall household food budgets matter independently of intrahousehold allocation.  Figure \ref{fig:ihds_eat_last} in the online appendix uses data from the nationally representative India Human Development Survey (IHDS) (2011) data to show that the proportion of women who report eating after the men in their households is similar across the five social groups \cite{DesaiVannemanIHDS2011}.

\section{Data and Methods}
 
The ideal data for measuring the proportion of women who are underweight before pregnancy would come from a national pregnancy monitoring system, where anthropometric measurements for the same person are taken before, during, and after pregnancy.  However, no such representative data for India exist.  Therefore, we estimate the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight from cross-sectional data \citep{coffey2015prepregnancy}.  We construct a nonparametric reweighting function based on observable characteristics of pregnant women, and apply it to representative cross-sectional data on the body mass index scores of nonpregnant women.  

We use the most recent Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) from India, the National Family Health Survey (2019-21) (NFHS-5). These data are publicly available from \texit{www.dhsprogram.com}.  The NFHS-5 is a clustered multistage sample survey that interviewed over 635,000 households and over 720,000 women between the ages of 15 and 49. 

\subsection{Key Variables}

{\bf{Body mass index.}} Body mass index (BMI) is a person's weight in kilograms divided by her height in meters squared.  The WHO classifies someone as ``underweight'' if her body mass index is less than 18.5 kg/m$^2$.  The NFHS-5 measured women's weights and heights at the time of the survey.  BMI is missing for 2.8\% of pregnant women and 3.4\% of nonpregnant women; these observations are dropped from the analysis.

{\bf{Pregnancy.}}  The NFHS-5 asks women ages 15 to 49 whether they are currently pregnant, and, if so, how many months pregnant they are. If the question on months of pregnancy is not answered, we use the amount of time between the last menstrual period and the date of the survey as a measure of gestational duration.\footnote{This substitution is made for less than 1\% of the pregnant sample.}  We drop women from the analysis who report pregnancies of one or two months duration because they are a select group.  Not all women are aware of early pregnancies and willing to report them.\footnote{Table \ref{regression3plusmopreg} in the online appendix shows that, among other differences, women who report being one or two months pregnant are more likely to be in the upper half of the wealth distribution than women who report three or more months of pregnancy. Table \ref{gestdur_distribution} in the online appendix shows that a smaller proportion of pregnant women report being one or two months pregnant relative to months three through eight.}

{\bf{Social group.}} The NFHS asks respondents whether they belong a Scheduled Caste, a Scheduled Tribe, an Other Backward Class, or a Forward Caste.  It also asks respondents their religion, listing Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, and ``others'' as options. Following the IHDS, which placed special emphasis on the study of social inequality, we combine the caste and religion variables into five mutually exclusive groups \citep{IHDS2005}.  

We code as Adivasi or Dalit those women who report belonging to a Scheduled Tribe or a Scheduled Caste, respectively, regardless of religion.  Women who report their religion as Muslim, and who do not belong to a Scheduled Caste or Tribe, are coded as Muslim. Hindu or Sikh women who report belonging to an Other Backward Class are coded as OBC.  Hindu women who do not belong to a Scheduled Tribe, a Scheduled Caste, or an Other Backward Class are coded as Forward caste.  Two percent of women interviewed by the NFHS-5 do not fall into any of these categories and are dropped from the analysis. They may identify as Christians, Jains, or as having no religious affiliation.

\subsection{Estimation of prepregnancy nutrition}

We apply a nonparametric reweighting function to the body mass index scores of nonpregnant women to make their distribution of the observable characteristics match that of pregnant women. This counterfactual distribution up-weights nonpregnant women who are more likely to become pregnant and down-weights those who are less likely to become pregnant, producing estimates that more closely approximate nutritional status at the beginning of pregnancy. This method addresses the challenge that we cannot directly observe the nutrition status of women at the time of conception. 
 
The reweighting function \(\psi\) is defined as \(\psi(x)=[f(x|P=1)]/[f(x|P=0)]\), the ratio of the weighted counts of pregnant to nonpregnant women in each bin \(x\), where a ``bin'' is a unique combination of the variables used in nonparametric reweighting. The reweighting function is used to estimate average BMI after being multiplied by each woman's sampling weight: \(\tilde{W}_i = W_i * \psi(x_i)\), where nonpregnant woman \(i\) in bin \(x_i\) has a sampling weight \(\tilde{W}_i\). The key assumption is that conditional on the binning variables, the distribution of BMI among nonpregnant women matches that of women who are beginning pregnancy.

We construct 2,560 unique bins from the intersection of the categorical variables described below.  For pregnant women, variables are coded according to what was true when the woman became pregnant.  For nonpregnant women, variables are constructed according to what was true at the time of the survey. These variable that we use were chosen because demographers have linked them to fertility \citep{martin1995women, bongaarts1987proximate, easterlin1971does, bongaarts1984proximate, das1987sex}.

\begin{itemize}
\item {\bf{not using modern contraception:}} We construct a binary variable that, for nonpregnant women, indicates that they did not use modern method of contraception in the 15 months prior to the survey.  For pregnant women, it indicates that they did not use modern contraception in the 15 months before the start of the pregnancy.  The information is obtained from the reproductive calendar, which offers a month by month history of the last 6 years of a woman’s experiences with childbearing and contraceptive use.  Modern contraception is defined as the pill, an IUD, injectables, a diaphragm, condoms, or male or female sterilization.\footnote{Perhaps surprisingly, about 5\% of pregnant women report having used a method of modern contraception in the month before they became pregnant.} 
\item {\bf{less than a primary education:}} This is a binary indicator for not having finished primary school.
\item {\bf{rural resident:}} A respondent is coded as a rural resident if she was interviewed in a rural primary sampling unit. 
\item {\bf{does not have a boy child:}} This is a binary indicator that a woman does not have any sons.
\item {\bf{age group:}} 15-19, 20-24, 25-29, 30-49 years old
\item {\bf{parity and birth spacing:}} We construct a categorical variable that captures both parity and birth spacing. Women who are pregnant with their first child are coded as parity 0, as are nonpregnant women who have no children.  For pregnant women, birth spacing is measured from the month of the previous live birth to the end of the current pregnancy, assuming that the current pregnancy will last nine months. For nonpregnant women, we assign the birth spacing that would be true if they became pregnant in the month of the survey.  Therefore, birth spacing is measured from the month of the previous live birth to nine months after the date of the interview. 
We construct the following categories: (1) Parity 0; (2) Parity 1, below 2 years spacing; (3) Parity 1, 2–3 years spacing; (4) Parity 1, 3+ years spacing; (5) Parity 2, 2 years spacing; (6) Parity 2, 2–3 years spacing; (7) Parity 2, 3+ years spacing; (8) Parity 3+, below 2 years spacing; (9) Parity 3+, 2–3 years spacing; and (10) Parity 3+, 3+ years spacing.
\item {\bf{houeshold wealth quartile:}} The NFHS-5 publishes a wealth index score based on a principal components analysis of household asset ownership (for example, a bicycle, a television, or a car) and housing characteristics (for example, source of drinking water, toilet facilities, and flooring materials) \citep{iips_icf_nfhs5_2022}. We split the wealth index score into quartiles. 
\end{itemize}

Bins that contain nonpregnant women, but not pregnant women, do not pose a problem for the analysis; they receive a weight of zero.  However, bins that contain pregnant women, but no nonpregnant women, must be dropped from the analysis. Table \ref{tab:sampledropped} in the online appendix shows the sample sizes of pregnant women used in the subgroup analyses, and the percent of pregnant women dropped because they are in a bin that contains no nonpregnant women.  

The reweighting procedure was conducted for the five social groups together, and within each social group.  When we decompose differences in the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight by social group, we compute estimates of prepregnancy underweight within each social group's parity and birth spacing bins, and within each social group's wealth quartile bins, for a total of 76 estimates of the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight.  

Standard errors are constructed using cluster bootstrapping.  We draw 30,131 clusters\footnote{This is slightly less than the number of primary sampling units in the NFHS-5 because we drop women from the analysis who do not belong to one of the social groups we study.} with replacement and compute the proportion of prepregnancy underweight in that sample, and repeat the procedure 1,000 times.  We present confidence intervals spanning the 5th and 95th percentiles of the distribution of resulting proportions of prepregnancy underweight.

\subsection{Kitagawa decomposition of differences in prepregnancy underweight between marginalized groups and Forward castes}

We conduct two Kitagawa decompositions \citep{kitagawa1955components}: the first decomposes gaps between marginalized and dominant groups using the parity and birth spacing categories, and the second decomposes gaps using wealth categories.  The overall gap in the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight between a marginalized group and Forward caste Hindus can be expressed as the sum of differences ``explained'' by the variable of interest and differences that remain ``unexplained:''

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kitagawa}
\Delta \equiv \bar Y^{g} - \bar Y^{f}
= \Delta_{explained} + \Delta_{unexplained}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation} \label{eq_explained}
    \Delta_{explained} = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \big(p_j^{g} - p_j^{f}\big)\,\frac{Y_j^{g} + Y_j^{f}}{2}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation} \label{eq_unexplained}
    \Delta_{unexplained} = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \big(Y_j^{g} - Y_j^{f}\big)\,\frac{p_j^{g} + p_j^{f}}{2}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item where \(j=1,...,J\) indexes categories of the decomposition variable (for parity and birth spacing J=10, for wealth quartiles J=4)
    \item \( \bar Y^g\) and \(\bar Y^f\) are the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight for groups \(g\) (marginalized group) and \(f\) (Forward caste Hindu)
    \item \(Y_j^g\) and \(Y_j^f\) are the means of prepregnancy underweight within category \(j\) of groups \(g\) and \(f\) respectively
    \item \(p_j^g\) and \(p_j^f\) are the proportions of women in each category \(j\) of the variable of interest
    
\end{itemize}

The percent of the gap explained by the variable of interest is computed as:

\begin{equation} \label{eq_pct}
    \frac{\Delta_{explained}}{\Delta_{total}}.
\end{equation}

\section{Results}

\subsection*{Descriptive statistics}

Table \ref{tab:sumstats} shows sample means for the variables we use in the reweighting decomposition, separately by social group and pregnancy status. Pregnant and nonpregnant women have different characteristics, on average. Pregnant women are younger and at lower parities: 43\% are aged 20–24 (versus 16\% of nonpregnant women), and 42\% have no prior births (versus 30\% of nonpregnant women). This highlights the potential biases of estimating prepregnancy underweight from the BMIs of nonpregnant women without adjustment for selection into pregnancy.

There are meaningful differences across social groups in these predictors of pregnancy. Nearly half of nonpregnant Adivasi women have less than primary education, compared to one in five nonpregnant Forward caste women. Nearly half of nonpregnant Adivasi women and more than a quarter of nonpregnant Dalit women, compared with only 10\% of nonpregnant Forward caste women have household wealth in the first (bottom) quartile. Adivasi and Dalit women are also overrepresented in the first quartile of household wealth among pregnant women.

To further demonstrate the value of adjusting for these covariates in the nonparametric reweighting analysis, Table \ref{tab:regression} in the online appendix shows the results of OLS linear probability models that regress pregnancy on the covariates.  The  models are estimated separately for each social group, and for all five groups combined. When looking at all social groups combined, each covariate, with the exception of being at parity 2 with 2-3 years of birth spacing, statistically significantly predicts pregnancy.

\subsection*{Prepregnancy underweight by social group}

Figure \ref{fig:mainresult} shows estimates of the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight by social group, and for all five groups combined. With the exception of Forward caste Hindus and Muslims, confidence intervals do not overlap for any social groups, indicating statistically significant differences in the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight at the 95\% level. The burden of prepregnancy underweight is highest among Adivasi women (28\%), followed by Dalit women (25\%), and OBC (23\%) women.  Forward caste Hindus (18\%) and Muslims (17\%) have a lower prevalence of prepregnancy underweight. Combining all five social groups, we find that 22\% of prepregnant women are underweight.\footnote{Our sample drops a small number of women who do not fall into one of the five social groups used in our analysis.  If we estimate prepregnancy underweight using the full NFHS-5 sample, we find that 22.36\% of prepregnant women in India are underweight.} Table \ref{specifications} in the online appendix shows that the results are robust to changes in the covariates used in the reweighting procedure. 

\subsection*{Social group differences in parity, birth spacing, and household wealth}

In order to motivate the Kitagawa decompositions, panels A, C, and E of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} show the distributions of pregnant women, by social group, into parity, birth spacing, and household wealth categories.  Panels B, D, and F of \ref{fig:sixpanel} show estimates of the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight, for all social groups combined, within parity, birth spacing, and household wealth categories.  

\subsubsection*{Parity}

Panel A of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} shows that Forward caste Hindu pregnant women are more likely than pregnant women from other social groups to be pregnant with their first child (parity 0). Almost half of pregnant Forward caste Hindu women are at parity 0, compared to 38-39\% of Muslim and Adivasi pregnant women. Muslim pregnant women are more likely than women from other social groups to be at higher parities, with 16\% at parity 3 or higher compared to 5\% among Forward caste Hindu women and 8\% among OBC women. Although present, fertility differences across social groups are not very large.\footnote{Table \ref{tab:tfr} in the online appendix complements the information in Panel A of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} by showing total fertility rates (TFR) for each of the five social groups based on births in the three years prior to the NFHS-5.  TFR for Forward caste Hindu women is 1.6, compared to 2.3 among Muslims, and 2.1 among Adivasis and Dalits.}  Fertility in India today is very close to replacement; higher order births are becoming rare.

Panel B of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} shows that the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight is highest among women who have no prior births.  One in four women who have no prior births are estimated to be underweight at the start of pregnancy, compared to about one in five women at parities one, two, and three plus.  It is consistent with the pattern observed in panel B that, as women progress through their childbearing careers, they are less likely to be underweight.  However, the cross-sectional nature of the data limits what we can conclude about the relationship between parity progression and maternal nutrition.  A confounding factor is that poorer women are more likely to be undernourished at any parity, and are more likely to progress to higher parities than wealthier women.  

\subsubsection*{Birth spacing}

Panel C of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} summarizes birth spacing for the 14,304 pregnant women in the NFHS-5 who had at least one prior birth.  Compared to women from other social groups, Dalit women were most likely to have birth spacing below two years.  28\% of Dalit pregnant women, compared to about 20\% of Forward caste Hindu, Adivasi, and Muslim pregnant women, are expected to give birth within two years of a prior birth.  Three or more years of birth spacing is modal for all social groups.  Fifty-five percent of Muslim pregnant women are expected to have at least three years birth spacing, compared to 40\% of Dalit pregnant women. Panel D of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} shows that the estimated prevalence of prepregnancy underweight varies little by birth spacing.

\subsubsection*{Household Wealth}

Panel E of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} shows stark differences in the distribution of household wealth across Indian social groups. Half of Adivasi pregnant women live in households in the lowest wealth quartile, compared to only 12\% of Forward caste Hindu pregnant women. By contrast, nearly 44\% of Forward caste Hindu pregnant women are in the top quartile, compared with fewer than one in five Dalit or Adivasi pregnant women. 

Panel F of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} estimates prepregnancy underweight by household wealth category.  In contrast to panels B and D, which show that the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight varies little by parity or by birth spacing, a clear and quantitatively important pattern emerges in panel F.  The prevalence of prepregnancy underweight is inversely related to household wealth.  Thirty percent of women in the poorest quartile are estimated to be underweight at the start of pregnancy, compared to 13\% in the top quartile.  

\subsection*{Decompositions of differences in prepregnancy underweight across social groups}\label{sec:kitagawa}

Table \ref{decomposition} summarizes Kitagawa decompositions of gaps in the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight between women from marginalized groups and Forward caste Hindu women.  The top row of Table \ref{decomposition} shows the raw gaps from Figure \ref{fig:mainresult}, the largest of which is the Adivasi-Forward gap of 10.2 percentage points.

Panel A shows that the parity and birth spacing categories explain little of the Forward caste advantage in prepregnancy underweight relative to Adivasi, Dalit, and OBC women (-1\% to 5\% of the gaps).  We note that there was no statistically significant gap to be explained between Muslim and Forward caste Hindu women.

Differences in the distributions of household wealth, in contrast, explain a large portion of the Forward caste Hindu advantage in prepregnancy underweight relative to Adivasis, Dalits, and OBCs: 73\%, 65\%, and 52\% of the respective gaps.  The decomposition of the gap between Muslims and Forward castes suggests that if Muslims had the same distribution of wealth as Forward caste Hindus, they might face a lower burden of prepregnancy underweight than Forward caste Hindus. 

\section{Discussion}

This paper contributes the most recent estimates of the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight for India and the first estimates by social group. Notably, we find that in 2019-21, more than one in five women in India was underweight at the beginning of pregnancy.  This represents progress in maternal health; the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight has come down by 50\% since 2005.  However, the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight remains high.  For comparison, in the United States, fewer than 5\% of women are underweight before pregnancy \citep{zong2022maternal}. Even among socially dominant Forward caste Hindus, the burden of prepregnancy underweight remains similar to what it was among all sub-Saharan African women two decades ago \citep{coffey2015prepregnancy}.  

Within India, the burden of maternal undernutrition falls disproportionately on Adivasi, Dalit, and OBC women.  Considering these groups' social and economic disadvantage, the finding may be unsurprising.  However, \cite{harris2017determinants}'s review article of intrahousehold food allocation among adults suggests that that oppressed caste households that are ``not severely food insecure'' may distribute food more equally between men and women that Forward caste Hindu households.  This may be because oppressed caste women are more likely to work outside the home than dominant caste womorryen, and so have greater autonomy within their homes \citep{chakrabarty1996gender, eswaran2013status}.  The frequency and nature of women's religious fasting may also differ across social groups.  However, our analysis of an IHDS (2011) question which asked ever-married women whether women eat with, or after, the men in their households, found no difference between Forward Caste Hindus, on the one hand, and Dalits or Adivasis, on the other.  Figure \ref{fig:ihds_eat_last} in the online appendix shows the proportion of women who report eating last, by social group.  Further research is needed to better understand whether there are social group differences in intrahousehold food allocation, and if so, why.  

The Kitagawa decompositions show that higher fertility and more closely spaced births among Adivasis, Dalits, and OBCs do not explain why they are more likely to be underweight before pregnancy than Forward caste Hindu women.  This is in part because the differences by social group in parity and birth spacing are small, and in part because these variables are only weakly correlated with prepregnancy underweight in the most recent Indian data. Differences in household wealth, in contrast, do explain large proportions of the gaps.  Future studies might investigate whether this is because wealthier households are able to afford food, because wealthier households can afford labor saving devices such as vehicles or washing machines, or because wealthier households offer better protection against disease.  

We might also ask whether wealthier households have more gender equitable food ways, independent of caste?  Data from the 1980s suggest that women reduce on food consumption by more than men during times of scarcity to ensure that men have the energy to work \citep{behrman1990intrahousehold}.  It is reasonable to assume that periods of scarcity are more common in poorer households than richer ones.  If so, discriminatory food practices could be more common in poor households than richer ones.  However, there has been substantial economic growth since this study was conducted, and a substantial reduction in the proportion of households reporting food insecurity \citep{deaton2009food}. 

We also note that a significant fraction of the gaps in the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight remains unexplained after accounting for differences in household wealth.  We expect that if we were able to measure differences in discrimination-related stress (such as with cortisol) or inflammation (such as with C-reactive protein), that might explain at least part of the remaining gaps.  This is an important hypothesis to consider, as the literature on racial disparities in birth outcomes in the United States increasingly points to racism-related stress as an important reason for higher rates of preterm birth and lower birth weights among Black infants \citep{hogue2005stress, braveman2015role, sosnaud2021cross}. Future research should aim to unpack how, and at what cost, childbearing-age women in India experience discrimination based on social group membership.  

Although not the focus of this paper, we note that Adivasi, Dalit, and OBC women do not compensate for prepregnancy underweight with higher weight gain during pregnancy.  The US Institute of Medicine (IOM) recommends that women who begin pregnancy underweight gain 28 to 40 pounds during pregnancy \citep{iom2009weightgain}. Women who have normal or overweight BMIs at the beginning of pregnancy should gain less.\footnote{We cite the IOM guidelines because the Government of India has not issued guidelines on weight gain in pregnancy. The IOM guidelines have also been endorsed by the United Kingdom's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence \citep{NICE2025}.}  Table \ref{tab:addnut} in the online appendix uses NFHS-5 data to show estimates of weight gain during pregnancy for each social group. The methods for deriving these estimates were developed by \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy} and are detailed in section \ref{sec:weightgain} of the online appendix.\footnote{Table \ref{tab:addnut} also computes the prevalence of prepregnancy obesity ($BMI>30$) in India and by social group, using the same method we used to compute the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight.  There are small disparities by social group: 11\% of Forward caste Hindu and Muslim women are obese before pregnancy, compared to 5\% of Adivasi women, 7\% of Dalit women, and 8\% of OBC women.  We include these estimates for completeness and to encourage future research on maternal nutrition.  Although obesity rates are increasing in India \citep{luhar2020forecasting}, obesity among women who are likely to become pregnant is relatively uncommon: 8\% of Indian women are estimated to be obese before pregnancy.} There is no statistically significant difference in weight gain during pregnancy across social groups.  For no group does the average estimated weight gain exceed 20 pounds. 

We note the similarity in the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight between Forward caste Hindu and Muslim women.  Despite social and economic disadvantage, Muslim women are no more likely to be underweight before pregnancy, and are less likely to be underweight after accounting for household wealth.  This result coheres with other research that finds advantages in Muslim women's and children's health relative to Hindus'. Could this be because Muslim households allocate food more equitably between young women and other family members than Hindus do?  \cite{desai2014muslim} analyzed IHDS (2005) data and found that Muslim women \emph{were} less likely to report eating after the men in their household than Hindu women.  Figure \ref{fig:ihds_eat_last} in the online appendix shows the same comparison using the IHDS 2011.  In the 2011 data, the difference in the proportion of Muslim and Hindu married women who report eating last was longer statistically significant.  Future qualitative research could find other ways of investigating the relationships among age, gender, and food allocation.

Can policies reduce maternal undernutrition and address disparities in prepregnancy underweight?  It may be more effective to focus on never-married women than married women.  The time interval between marriage and conception is short: on average, women who had their first births in the three years before the NFHS-5 were married for only 17.23 months before conception.  \cite{banerjee2024persistent} study a cash transfer program in West Bengal in which girls teenage girls received Rs. 1,000 for every year they remained unmarried and in school. They received Rs. 25,000 if they remained in school and unmarried until age 18.  The program delayed marriage and made girls less likely to say that (hypothetical) domestic violence was justified.  Such program might impact girls' nutrition as well.  Because the marginal impact of a fixed amount of cash is greater for poorer girls, such a program would likely improve equity as well.  

Our research also underscores the potential of the 2013 National Food Security Act's maternity entitlements to improve maternal nutrition.  The law promised nearly all pregnant women a Rs. 6,000 benefit, but in practice, only a fraction of the funds that would be needed for a universal program have been allocated.  In 2020, allocation to maternity entitlements was only enough to cover about 1 in 7 women who gave birth \cite{dreze2021derailed}.  Maternity entitlements are often restricted to the first child and are conditional on the woman receiving care from government health clinics \citep{kalra2020birth}.  If fully funded, indexed for inflation, and given at the start of pregnancy, cash transfers during pregnancy could help address India's high burden of prepregnancy underweight \citep{coffey2016underweight}. Policymakers interested in addressing disparities in maternal nutrition might also consider increasing the amount of the transfer to women from marginalized groups.

Finally, this study highlights the value of representative, longitudinal monitoring data on health in pregnancy for India.  The cross sectional data that we analyze permit the construction of synthetic cohorts that yield valuable insights into maternal nutrition, and disparities in maternal nutrition.  Yet, they do not allow for individual level analyses.  For example, we do not know what fraction of women who begin pregnancy underweight compensate for low prepregnancy body mass with adequate weight gain.  We do not know how many women who begin pregnancy underweight also suffer from anemia.  We do not know how many women who begin pregnancy underweight have a preterm or low birthweight infant.  Considering that India is now the country with the highest number of births globally, investments in Indian health data will improve global health.

\section{S.\arabic{section} Measuring weight gain during pregnancy} \label{sec:weightgain}

\subsection{S.\arabic{section}.\arabic{subsection} Weight gain method 1} \label{sec:weightgainm1}

The first method of estimating weight gain starts by estimating monthly weight gain of pregnant women at 3+ months gestational duration as $\hat{\beta}$ from an OLS regression of the form
\begin{equation}
    weight_i = \alpha + \beta month_i + \tau C_i + \epsilon_i
\end{equation}

where $weight_i$ is the weight in kilograms of pregnant woman $i$. $month_i$ is month of gestation, calculated as described in the methods section, and $C_i$ are controls, including women's current ages (single year), years of education, number of living children, urban or rural residence, wealth quintile, as well as state-month fixed effects.

Assuming women gain 10 times more weight in their second and third trimesters than in their first, we estimate weight gain for a full-term pregnancy as 
\begin{equation}
    \hat{weight gain} = 1.1\times6\times\hat{\beta}
\end{equation}

In table A1, the 95\% confidence intervals for weight gain method 1 are calculated as $1.1\times6\times\hat{\beta} \pm 1.96\times se_{\hat{\beta}}$.

\subsection{S.\arabic{section}.\arabic{subsection} Weight gain method 2} \label{sec:weightgainm2}


The second method of estimating weight gain takes the difference of the average weight of pregnant women at gestational duration of 9 months or more and the average prepregnancy weight using the nonparametric reweighting method. 


\begin{equation}
    \hat{weight gain} = (\bar{W}_9-\bar{W}_0)+(0.5)\hat{\beta}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{W}_9$ is the average weight of pregnant women at gestational duration of 9 months or more, and $\bar{W}_0$ is the average prepregnancy weight using the nonparametric reweighting method. To account for additional weight gain between the time of survey and the end of pregnancy for women of gestational duration of 9 months or more, we assume these women are at the midpoint of their last month of pregnancy and add the accordingly scaled $\hat{\beta}$ from the regression in weight gain method 1.

This offers a benchmark to method 1, which may be inaccurate if the assumption is false that women gain 10 times more weight in their second and third trimesters than in their first, or if gestational durations are incorrectly reported.
