\title{Fertility, Economic Status, and Maternal Nutrition among Indian Social Groups}
\author{authors}
\date{\today}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Maternal undernutrition remains an important threat to women's health and an important cause of child mortality and morbidity worldwide. Prior research shows that maternal undernutrition is particularly high in India. This paper estimates prepregnancy underweight using India's National Family Health Survey (2019-21) and compares prepregnancy underweight across social groups.  We use nonparametric reweighting to show that the burden of prepregnancy underweight in India has declined in recent decades -- from about 42\% in 2005 to about 22\% in 2019-21.  We find that prepregnancy underweight is about 55\% higher among Adivasis and 40\% higher among Dalits than it is among Forward Caste Hindus and Muslims.  Fertility and birth spacing, factors typically assumed to contribute to maternal undernutrition, do not explain differences in prepregnancy underweight across social groups. Instead, accounting for differences in household economic status explains much, but not all, of the prepregnancy underweight gap between Adivasis and Forward Castes, and between Dalits and Forward Castes. Accounting for wealth differences reveals that despite multidimensional disadvantage, Muslims have lower rates of prepregnancy underweight than Forward Caste Hindus.  
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Maternal nutrition is important to women's health, influencing both their pregnancy and postpartum experiences, and their survival chances.  In undernourished women, limited fat, muscle, and micronutrient reserves combine with reduced immune function and blood volume, making it difficult to meet the energy demands of pregnancy.  The inter-generational transmission of health is perhaps nowhere more evident than it is during pregnancy: undernourished mothers are more likely to have low birth weight and preterm babies, who face a higher risk of death than babies born to better nourished mothers \citep{ahmed2013global}.  Among surviving children, those born to undernourished mothers are more likely to be malnourished themselves.

India has historically had a high burden of maternal undernutrition, with significant consequences for women's and children's health. \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy} found that, in 2005, 42\% of Indian women began pregnancy with a body mass index less than 18.5, the World Health Organization cutoff for chronic energy deficiency \citep{who_nlis_malnutrition_women_2021}.  This was nearly two and half times the rate of prepregnancy underweight among women in sub-Saharan Africa during the same period.  According to \cite{ahmed2013global}, maternal undernutrition is a major cause of intrauterine growth restriction in South Asia. Using data from the Sample Registration System and the Million Deaths study, \cite{fadel2017changes} find that low birth weight and prematurity (listed as a single cause because they are difficult to disentangle in verbal autopsy) is now the leading cause of child mortality in India.  

This paper presents updated estimates of prepregnancy underweight in India and contributes the first estimates of prepregnancy underweight by social group. It joins a growing literature on health disparities among caste and religious groups in India, where national averages mask stark group-level inequalities \citep{vyas2022social, gupta2020caste, coffey2019local, lopalo2019consequences, geruso2014sanitation, bora2019persistent}. We find that about 22\% of Indian women have BMIs of less than 18.5 before pregnancy, but that this number is higher among Adivasis (28\%) and Dalits (25\%) than it is among forward-caste Hindus (18\%) and Muslims who are not from oppressed castes (17\%).  

This study investigates the reasons for these disparities.  We first consider whether differences in fertility and birth spacing can account for social group differences in prepregnancy underweight.  We test this hypothesis because prior research on maternal and infant health emphasizes the role of high parity and short birth spacing in ``depleting'' mothers' nutritional status \citep{winkvist1992new}.  Using a Kitagawa decomposition, we find that differences in parity and birth spacing between forward and oppressed caste women account for very little of the gaps that we document.  

Second, we consider whether differences in economic resources can explain the gaps that we observe.  We find that the distributions of prepregnant women into wealth quartiles can explain about 70\% of the forward caste-Adivasi gap, and about 65\% of the forward caste-Dalit gap.  When we apply a Kitawaga decomposition by wealth category to the forward caste-Muslim gap, where there was no original gap to be explained, we find that after accounting for wealth differences, Muslim women are less likely to be underweight before pregnancy than forward caste women.  This is consistent with research on health disparities that document health advantages among Muslims \citep{geruso2018neighborhood, sharma_gupta_2025}.  We note that although differences in the distribution of women into wealth categories can explain a majority of the gaps we observe, large fractions of the gaps remain unexplained. 

In the next section, we present background information on maternal and child health in India, on Indian social groups and population health, and on the causes of maternal undernutrition.   We then describe our data source -- the National Family Health Survey (2019-21) -- and our methods.  In brief, we compute prepregnancy underweight using a non-parametric reweighting technique to identify a group of nonpregnant women who are especially likely to become pregnant.  We apply this technique to the population of Indian women as a whole, and to each social group separately.  Finally, we investigate candidate explanations for group-level disparities in maternal underweight.

\section{Background}

\subsection{Indian social groups and population health}

Caste and religion are important axes of social inequality in India \citep{vaid2014caste, kumar2016explaining, varshney2002ethnic, majid2015indian}. A person's \textit{jati}, or caste sub-group, influences their social interactions, marriage, occupation, and life chances \citep{deshpande2011grammar, thorat2012blocked}. Although caste is a concept most closely associated with Hinduism, people from all religious groups are stratified along caste lines. 

In an effort to address historical and ongoing discrimination, the central and state governments of India have affirmative action for people from marginalized \textit{jatis} in higher education and government jobs. Affirmative action policies were written into the Indian Constitution, which recognizes three historically disadvantaged groups. Scheduled Castes (SC) comprise \textit{jatis} that were traditionally considered ``untouchable.''  Both historically and today, Scheduled Castes do stigmatized work such as leather work, cremation, and the removal of human feces.  People from Scheduled Castes refer to themselves as \textit{Dalit}, or ``oppressed people.''  We use this term in our paper. Other Backward Classes (OBC) are \textit{jatis} considered socially, economically, or educationally deprived compared to the dominant, or ``forward,'' castes.  ``Backward'' is a term used in the Constitution; we use the abbreviation ``OBC'' in this paper with no normative implications. Scheduled Tribes (ST) refer to India’s indigenous population, who face persistent disadvantage due to geographic isolation and exclusion from state services.  To refer to Scheduled Tribes, we use the term ``Adivasi,'' a term of self-identification that means ``early inhabitants.''  In referring to socially dominant Hindu \textit{jatis} that are ineligible for affirmative action, we follow the National Family Health Survey in using the term ``forward castes.''

Muslims, India's largest religious minority, comprise 14\% of the population.  Historically and today, Muslims face social exclusion and targeted violence.  Political movements linking Indian nationalism with Hinduism are central to the marginalization of Muslims \citep{farooqui2020political}. Muslims belong to Dalit, Adivasi, OBC, and forward caste jatis \citep{ahmad2007recognition}, but are typically not eligible for affirmative action policies, with the exception of OBC Muslims in some Indian states \cite{hasan2018muslims, hasan2022wider}.   

The inequalities created by caste and religion shape population health in India.  For example, \cite{gupta2022large} find that Dalits, Adivasis, and Muslims have lower life expectancies than forward caste Hindus.  \cite{gupta2020caste} find higher prevalences of self-reported depression and anxiety among Dalits and Adivasis than among forward caste Hindus.  \cite{coffey2019local} document gaps in child height among children from SC, OBC, ST, and forward caste backgrounds.

Social marginalization does not always correspond with health disadvantage, however.  Research finds better health for Muslim children and women on some indicators.  \cite{geruso2018neighborhood} show that Muslim infants are more likely to survive because they are exposed to better neighborhood sanitation, on average, than Hindu infants. \cite{sharma_gupta_2025} find that Muslim women are less likely to experience domestic violence than Hindu women, and that the difference can largely be explained by lower use of alcohol among Muslim men.

\subsection{Maternal and child nutrition in India}

In the late 1990s, researchers noticed that South Asian children had higher rates of malnutrition than children in sub-Saharan Africa, despite being less poor, on average \citep{ramalingaswami1996commentary, underwood2002health}.  They posited that maternal malnutrition, related to the low social status of Indian women, contributed to a high prevalence of low birthweight and widespread child malnurition.  Using Demographic and Health Surveys from the early 2000s, \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy} found that 42\% of Indian women were underweight before pregnancy, and the average woman gained so little weight during pregnancy that she weighed less at the end of pregnancy than the average woman in sub-Saharan Africa weighed at the beginning of pregnancy.  Evidence for the link between women's nutrition and social status has also been published \citep{coffey2022mothers, hathi2021women}.  

This paper's finding that 22\% of Indian women are underweight before pregnancy therefore indicates rapid improvement in maternal nutrition in recent years.  We note, however, that the rate of pre-pregnancy underweight among forward caste Hindu women, the most advantaged social group we study, is high compared to other settings.  At 18\%, prepregnancy underweight among forward caste Hindu women is similar to what it was among sub-Saharan African women two decades ago \citep{coffey2015prepregnancy}.  In the United States, fewer than 5\% of women are underweight before pregnancy \citep{zong2022maternal}.  

\subsection{Fertility, household economic status, and nutrition among Indian social groups}

Research on maternal health in low and middle income countries studies ``maternal depletion,'' the idea that higher-order births and low spacing between births can leave women nutritionally vulnerable \citep{winkvist1992new, king2003risk, shell2004maternal}.  Can differences in fertility and birth spacing explain differences in prepregnancy underweight across Indian social groups?  Higher fertility among Muslims compared to Hindus has been widely studied \citep{dharmalingam2004pervasive, bhat2005role, james2005accelerated} and has featured prominently in political discourse \citep{jeffery2002population, singh2020numbering}.  Although less well studied, each National Family Health Survey finds higher fertility rates among oppressed castes than among forward castes. The literature also notes differences in birth spacing across caste and religious groups \citep{kundu2025short, maity2017comparing, bhalotra2008birth, singh2024wealth}.  

Whether differences in fertility and birth spacing will explain differences in prepregnancy underweight will depend both on the magnitude of fertility and birth spacing differences across groups, and on the relationship between parity progression and birth spacing, on the one hand, and nutrition, on the other. \cite{coffey2021neonatal} provide suggestive evidence that, in India, higher parity births may not lead to maternal depletion.  They find that controlling for siblingship size, higher-parity births are less likely to die in the first month of life than lower-parity births.  They posit that this may be because women gain weight with successive pregnancies as a result of increases in social status that accompany ageing and parity progression. 

There are several pathways through which household wealth protects against undernutrition: it allows people to buy more and higher quality food \citep{raghunathan2021affordability}, they are more likely to have tools and technology that reduce their energy output \citep{deaton2009food}, and they may be able to afford cleaner water and better sanitation, which reduces energy loss to disease \citep{bhutta2008works}.  Since marginalized castes are more likely to be poor, and to remain poor over time, than forward castes \citep{thorat2012has, thorat2017escaping}, we expect differences in household wealth to explain differences in undernutrition across Indian social groups.  Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes have household incomes up to 30\% less than the national average, and OBCs and Muslims own up to 10\% less \citep{bharti2018wealth}. 



We note that the relationship between economic status and nutrition is not straightforward.  Calorie consumption in India declined between the 1980s and early 2000s, despite economic growth and reductions in poverty, and despite the fact that underweight rates among Indian adults and children remained high compared to low- and middle-income countries \citep{deaton2009food}.  The low social status of women adds nuance to the relationship.  Data from the 1980s found that, more so than the nutrient intake of men in the same household, women's nutrient intake declined when food prices rose \citep{behrman1990intrahousehold}.  The 2011 Indian Human Development Survey found that in a quarter of Indian households, women are expected to eat after men \citep{hathi2021women}.  Figure \ref{fig:ihds_eat_last} in the online appendix shows that the proportion of  women who regularly eat last in their household is similar across the five social groups that we study.

\section{Data and Methods}
 
The ideal data for measuring the proportion of women who are underweight before pregnancy would come from a national pregnancy monitoring system tracking women before, during, and after pregnancy.  However, no such representative, longitudinal data exist.  Therefore, we use \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy}'s method of estimating the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight from cross-sectional data.  We construct a nonparametric reweighting function based on observable characteristics of pregnant women, and apply it to representative cross-sectional data on body mass index scores among non-pregnant women.  

We use the most recent Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) from India, the National Family Health Survey (2019-21) (NFHS 5). These data are publicly available from dhsprogram.com.  NFHS 5 is a clustered multistage sample survey that interviewed over 635,000 households and over 720,000 women between the ages of 15 and 49. 

\subsection{Variables}

{\bf{Body mass index.}} Body mass index (BMI) is a person's weight in kilograms divided by her height in meters squared.  The WHO classifies someone as ``underweight'' if her body mass index is less than 18.5.  The NFHS 5 measured women's weights and heights at the time of the survey.  BMI is missing for 2.8\% of pregnant women and 3.4\% of non-pregnant women; these observations are dropped from the analysis.

{\bf{Pregnancy.}}  The NFHS asks all women ages 15 to 49 whether they are currently pregnant, and, if so, how many months pregnant they are.  We code a woman as pregnant if she reports being three or more months pregnant.\footnote{If the question on months of pregnancy is not answered, we use reported time since last menstrual period as a measure of gestational duration. Months of pregnancy is missing for only 0.06\% of the pregnant sample.}  We drop women from the analysis who report pregnancies of one or two months duration because not all women are aware of early pregnancies and willing to report them.  Table \ref{regression3plusmopreg} in the online appendix shows that, among other differences, women who report one or two months of pregnancy are more likely to be in the upper half of the wealth distribution than women who report three or more months of pregnancy. Table \ref{gestdur_distribution} in the online appendix shows further evidence that reporting one or two months of gestation is selective: 4\% of pregnant women report being one month pregnant and 11\% of pregnant women report being two months pregnant.  The proportion of pregnant women reporting months of pregnancy three through eight is fairly constant at about 13\%.

{\bf{Social group.}} The NFHS asks respondents whether they belong a Scheduled Caste, a Scheduled Tribe, an Other Backward Class, or a Forward Caste.  It also asks respondents their religion, listing Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, and ``others'' as options.  Following the India Human Development Survey (IHDS) \citep{IHDS2005}, we combine these variables into five mutually exclusive, socially meaningful groups.  Adivasi and Dalit women are those who report belonging to a Scheduled Tribe or a Scheduled Caste, respectively, regardless of religion.  Muslim women are those who report their religion as Muslim and who do not belong to a Scheduled Caste or Tribe. OBC women are Hindu or Sikh women who report belonging to an Other Backward Class.  Forward caste women are Hindus who do not belong to a Scheduled Tribe, a Scheduled Caste, or Other Backward Class.  2.3\% of the women interviewed by the NFHS 5 do not fall into any of these categories.  They may identify as Christians, Jains, or as not having a religious or caste identity.  These women are dropped from the analysis for ease of interpretation. 

{\bf{Predictors of maternal undernutrition and pregnancy used in nonparametric reweighting.}} Our method for estimating pre-pregnancy underweight, described below, involves matching non-pregnant to pregnant women on variables that predict both pregnancy and body weight.  We construct the following variables for use in the nonparametric reweighting analysis:
\begin{itemize}
\item {\bf{not using modern contraception:}} A binary indicator that the woman is not using a modern method at the time of the survey (non-pregnant women) or was not using modern contraception in the month before becoming pregnant (pregnant women).   Modern contraception is defined as the pill, an IUD, injectables, a diaphragm, condoms, or male or female sterilization. 
\item {\bf{less than a primary education:}} A binary indicator for not having finished primary school.
\item {\bf{rural resident:}} A respondent is a rural resident if she was interviewed in a rural primary sampling unit. 
\item {\bf{does not have a boy child:}} A binary indicator that a woman does not have any sons.
\item {\bf{age group:}} 15-19, 20-24, 25-29, 30-49
\item {\bf{parity and birthspacing:}} A categorical variable that captures both parity (0, 1, 2, 3+) and birth spacing (below 2 years, 2-3 years, 3 or more years). For pregnant women, birth spacing is measured from the month of the previous live birth to the end of the current pregnancy, assuming that the pregnancy will last nine months. For non-pregnant women, spacing is measured from the previous live birth to nine months after the date of the interview. This gives the following categories: (1) No births; (2) 1 birth, below 2-year spacing; (3) 1 birth, 2–3 year spacing; (4) 1 birth, 3+ year spacing; (5) 2 births, below 2-year spacing; (6) 2 births, 2–3 year spacing; (7) 2 births, 3+ year spacing; (8) 3+ births, below 2-year spacing; (9) 3+ births, 2–3 year spacing; and (10) 3+ births, 3+ year spacing.
\item {\bf{wealth quartile:}} The NFHS publishes a wealth index score for each household based on a principal component analysis of whether or not the household owns set of assets (for example, a bicycle, a television, or a car), and housing characteristics (for example, source of drinking water, toilet facilities, and flooring materials) \citep{iips_icf_nfhs5_2022}. We split the wealth index score into quartiles. More information about the variables and methods used in creation of the NFHS wealth index can be found in section 1 of the appendix.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Estimation of prepregnancy nutrition}

Following \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy}, we apply a nonparametric reweighting function to the body mass index scores of nonpregnant women so that their distribution of the observable characteristics described above matches that of pregnant women. This counterfactual distribution up-weights nonpregnant women who are more likely to become pregnant and down-weights those who are less likely to become pregnant, producing estimates that more closely approximate women's nutritional status at the beginning of pregnancy. This method addresses the challenge that we cannot directly observe the nutrition status of women at the time of conception. The reweighting procedure was conducted for the five social groups together, and within each social group.  When we decompose differences in the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight by social group, we compute estimates of prepregnancy underweight within each social group's birth spacing/parity bins, and within each social group's wealth quartile bins, for a total of 76 estimates of the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight.   

The reweighting function \(\psi\) is defined as \(\psi(x)=[f(x|P=1)]/[f(x|P=0)]\), the ratio of the weighted counts of pregnant to nonpregnant women in each bin \(x\), where a ``bin'' is a unique combination of the predictors.  There are 2,560 bins, though not all of them contain both pregnant and nonpregnant women.  Bins that contain to nonpregnant women, but not pregnant women, do not pose a problem for the analysis; they receive a weight of zero.  However, bins that contain pregnant women, but no nonpregnant women, must be dropped from the analysis.  Table \ref{tab:sampledropped} in the online appendix shows the sample sizes of pregnant women for each of the subgroup analyses, and the percent of pregnant women dropped when computing the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight in that subgroup.  We drop no more than 3\% of the pregnant sample in 73 of 76 estimates. The reweighting function is used to estimate average BMI after being multiplied by each woman's sampling weight: \(\tilde{W}_i = W_i * \psi(x_i)\) where nonpregnant woman \(i\) in bin \(x_i\) has a sampling weight \(\tilde{W}_i\). 

Standard errors for the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight are constructed using cluster bootstrapping.  We draw 30,131 clusters (slightly less than the number of primary sampling units in the NFHS 5 because we drop women from the analysis who do not belong to one of the social groups we study) with replacement and compute the proportion of prepregnancy underweight in that sample, and repeat the procedure 1,000 times.  We present confidence intervals spanning the 5th and 95th percentiles of the distribution of resulting proportions of prepregnancy underweight.

\subsection{Kitagawa decomposition of differences in prepregnancy underweight between marginalized groups and forward castes}

To better understand the gaps in prepregnancy underweight between marginalized groups and forward castes, we conduct two Kitagawa-style decompositions \cite{kitagawa1955components}.  The first decomposes gaps by the parity and birth spacing variable described above.  The second decomposes gaps by wealth quartile.  

% To understand if gaps in prepregnancy underweight between marginalized groups and forward castes can be be explained by differences in covariates of maternal health, we conduct two Kitagawa-style decompositions \cite{kitagawa1955components}. The first decomposes gaps by the parity and birth spacing variable described above.  The second decomposes gaps by wealth quartile.  







Following \cite{kitagawa1955components}, we decompose differences in the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kitagawa}
\Delta \equiv \bar Y^{f} - \bar Y^{g}
= \Delta_{explained} + \Delta_{unexplained}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{explained} = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \big(p_j^{f} - p_j^{g}\big)\,\frac{Y_j^{f} + Y_j^{g}}{2}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{unexplained} = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \big(Y_j^{f} - Y_j^{g}\big)\,\frac{p_j^{f} + p_j^{g}}{2}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item where \(i=1,...,J\) indexes categories of the decomposition variable (e.g. parity/spacing, for which J=10, and wealth, for which J=4)
    \item \( \bar Y^f\) and \(\bar Y^g\) are the overall means for groups \(f\) (forward caste) and \(g\) (marginalized group)
    \item \(Y_i^f\) and \(Y_i^g\) are the means of prepregnancy underweight within category \(j\) of groups \(f\) and \(g\) respectively
    \item \(p_i^f\) and \(p_i^g\) are the proportions of women in category \(j\)
    
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}

\subsection*{Sample means of pregnancy covariates (Table \ref{sumstats})}

Table \ref{sumstats} shows sample means of the covariates of pregnancy later used in the reweighting procedure, illustrating two sets of contrasts.

First, pregnant and nonpregnant women have systematically different characteristics. Pregnant women are much younger and at lower parities - 43\% are aged 20–24 (versus 16\% of nonpregnant women), and 42\% have no prior births (versus 30\%). In contrast, nonpregnant women in the sample are much older and at higher parity: 25\% have had 3 or more births with over 3 years of birth spacing from their last birth, compared to only 4\% of pregnant women. This is likely to be the end of childbearing for most women, as total fertility rates are close to 2 for all social groups as seen in table \ref{tfr}. The differences of these means across pregnancy status highlights the the potential biases that come with estimating prepregnancy outcomes simply based on nonpregnant women. This motivates our inclusion of these covariates in the reweighting procedure to alter the distribution of nonpregnant women to match that of pregnant women in our sample.

Second, there are stark differences across social groups, highlighting inequality that reflects in comparisons of prepregnancy nutrition. Nearly half of Adivasi nonpregnant women have less than primary education, compared to only 21\% of forwards caste nonpregnant women. Women in the the lowest wealth quartile makes up nearly half of Adivasi and 27\% of Dalit nonpregnant women, compared to only 10\% of forwards caste nonpregnant women. These patterns are also consistent for pregnant women.


\subsection*{Regressions predicting pregnancy (Table \ref{regression})}

The covariates used in the reweighting procedure are characteristics that demography research has linked to fertility and are also plausibly correlated with body mass [28-34 in \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy}]. To test this, we regress an indicator of pregnancy on these covariates using the full sample of pregnant and nonpregnant women and observe if they are predictive of pregnancy risk. Table \ref{regression} reports results from these linear probability regressions conducted separately for each social group in our analysis. Almost all coefficients are significant in predicting pregnancy, highlighting their importance in being used in reweighting.

For instance, women that didn't use modern contraception when they were most recently nonpregnant were up to 6.5\% more likely to be pregnant across all social groups. Also, women who did not yet have a son were up to 2.6\% more likely to be pregnant across all social groups. Considering parity and birth spacing simultaneously is also important - women who had 1 birth between 2 and 3 years ago were 10.4\% more likely to be pregnant for all social groups combined.

The less predictive, but still statistically significant covariates of pregnancy were wealth quartile, less than primary education, and rural resident. We retain wealth so that we can get accurate pre-pregnancy underweight estimates by wealth quartile. However, we consider reweighting specifications without rural residence and less and primary education, and with finer age bins in Table \ref{specifications}. We find that prepregnancy underweight estimates are relatively stable across these specifications, confirming the validity of our method.

\subsection*{Prepregnancy underweight by social group (Figure \ref{fig:mainresult})}

Figure \ref{fig:mainresult} shows the estimates of prepregnancy underweight by social group. Confidence intervals don't overlap for any two social groups besides forward caste and Muslim, confirming statistically significant social inequalities in prepregnancy underweight at the 95\% level. The burden of prepregnancy undernutrition is highest among Adivasi women: 28\% are underweight at the onset of pregnancy. Dalit and OBC prepregnant women are also at higher risk, with 25\% and 23\% underweight, respectively. On the other hand, prepregnancy underweight is lowest among the forward caste (18\%) and Muslim (17\%) subgroups. Though prepregnancy underweight is lower among Muslim women than forward caste women, this difference is not statistically significant. Across all five groups, 22\% of prepregnant women are underweight, suggesting a relatively larger representation of Adivasi, Dalit, and OBC women among India's prepregnant population. 

\subsection*{Prepregnancy underweight by parity, birth spacing, and wealth quartile (Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel})}

Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} shows distributions of pregnant women and prepregnancy underweight estimates by parity, birth spacing, and wealth quartile categories. These categories are central to understanding social group disparities in prepregnancy health - repeated childbearing with limited recovery time may lead to maternal depletion, and poverty constrains access to adequate nutrition and healthcare.

\subsubsection*{Parity}

Among currently pregnant women, those who are forward caste were most likely among all social groups to be on their first or second birth - almost half are at parity 1, compared to 38-39\% of Muslim and Adivasi pregnant women (Panel A). On the other hand, Muslim pregnant women are the most likely to be at higher parities, with 16\% at parity 4 or higher compared to only 5\% among forward caste and 8\% of OBC pregnant women. This underscores how fertility patterns differ by social group, and is reflected in differences of total fertility rates in table \ref{tfr}.

Prepregnancy underweight is highest among women who have no prior births at 26\%, and drops steeply at parity 2 and 3, where in both cases 19\% of prepregnant women are underweight (Panel B). At parities of 4 or higher, 21\% are underweight.

\subsubsection*{Birth spacing}

Low birth spacing (below 2 years) was most common in the Dalit subgroup of currently pregnant women (28\%) compared to around 19\% among the Adivasi and Muslim subgroups (Panel C). Healthy birth spacing (above 3 years) was most common in the Muslim and forward caste subgroup of currently pregnant women at around 55\%. Prepregnancy underweight is highest among women who have had 2-3 years since their most recent birth (25\%) compared to less than 2 years (23\%) and above 3 years (21\%).

\subsubsection*{Wealth}

Wealth distributions reveal stark inequality (Panel E). Half of Adivasi pregnant women fall in the lowest wealth quartile, compared to only 12\% of forward caste pregnant women. By contrast, nearly 44\% of forward caste women are in the top quartile, compared with fewer than one in five Dalit or Adivasi women. Prepregnancy underweight decreases steadily with wealth, from roughly 30\% in the poorest quartile to 13\% in the richest (declining about 5 percentage points per quartile).

\subsection*{Decompositions of social group differences (Table 3)}

Table \ref{decomposition} shows decompositions of the difference between underweight among forward caste prepregnant women and prepregnant women from other social groups.

Panel A shows that a combined parity and birthspacing variable explains very little of the forward caste advantage in prepregnancy nutrition compared to Adivasi, Dalit, and OBC prepregnant women (-2\% to 4\%). Although a majority of the gap between Muslim and Forward caste prepregnancy underweight is explained by the combined parity and birthspacing variable, the gap is statistically insignificant to begin with.

On the other hand, wealth quartile explains a large portion of the forward caste advantage in prepregnancy underweight relative to the Adivasi, Dalit, and OBC subgroups - between 49\% and 69\%. On the other hand, the small and statistically insignificant difference between forward caste and Muslim prepregnancy underweight becomes 185\% larger when standardizing for wealth quartile. These results are all consistent with the economic advantage of forward caste Indians and the positive relationship between wealth and health.

\subsection{S.1 Wealth index construction in NFHS 5}

The wealth index is constructed as the first principal component from a set of household-level indicators of asset ownership and household level facilities \citep{DHSIndiaWealth2019}:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Water and sanitation:} source of drinking water (piped, tubewell/borehole, dug well, spring, rainwater, tanker/cart supply, surface water, bottled/RO) and type of toilet facility (flush to sewer/septic/pit/other, ventilated improved pit/composting/dry toilet, open defecation), including whether facilities are shared.
\item \textbf{Drainage and infrastructure:} presence and type of drainage (closed, open, none) and availability of electricity.
\item \textbf{Household durables:} assets such as mattress, pressure cooker, chair, bed, table, fan, radio, television, sewing machine, phone (mobile/landline), internet, computer, refrigerator, air conditioner/cooler, washing machine, watch/clock.
\item \textbf{Transport and productive assets:} bicycle, motorcycle/scooter, animal-drawn cart, car, water pump, thresher, tractor.
\item \textbf{Cooking fuel:} electricity, LPG/natural gas, biogas, kerosene, coal/lignite, charcoal, wood, crop waste, dung cakes, and related categories (including “no food cooked”).
\item \textbf{Housing materials:} main floor (e.g., earth/dung, stone, ceramic tiles, cement, polished stone/marble), main roof (e.g., thatch/grass, metal, cement/concrete, tiles, slate), and main walls (e.g., mud, bamboo/cane, stone with mud, burnt brick, cement/concrete, blocks, metal sheeting).
\item \textbf{Livestock, land, housing tenure, services:} ownership of livestock (e.g., cattle, goats, sheep, poultry), bank account, home ownership, land ownership, and presence of domestic staff.
\item \textbf{Crowding (continuous):} number of household members per sleeping room.

\end{itemize}

The resulting wealth score is equally assigned to each household member, individuals are ranked nationally, and the distribution is split into four quartiles to create our wealth index.


\subsection*{S.2 Measuring weight gain during pregnancy}


\subsubsection{S.2.1 Weight gain method 1}

The first method of estimating weight gain starts by estimating monthly weight gain of pregnant women at 3+ months gestational duration as $\hat{\beta}$ from an OLS regression of the form
\begin{equation}
    weight_i = \alpha + \beta month_i + \tau C_i + \epsilon_i
\end{equation}

where $weight_i$ is the weight in kilograms of pregnant woman $i$. $month_i$ is month of gestation, calculated as described in the methods section, and $C_i$ are controls, including women's current ages (single year), years of education, number of living children, urban or rural residence, wealth quintile, as well as state-month fixed effects.

Assuming women gain 10 times more weight in their second and third trimesters than in their first, we estimate weight gain for a full-term pregnancy as 
\begin{equation}
    \hat{weight gain} = 1.1\times6\times\hat{\beta}
\end{equation}

In table A1, the 95\% confidence intervals for weight gain method 1 are calculated as $1.1\times6\times\hat{\beta} \pm 1.96\times se_{\hat{\beta}}$.

\subsubsection{S.2.2 Weight gain method 2}

The second method of estimating weight gain takes the difference of the average weight of pregnant women at gestational duration of 9 months or more and the average prepregnancy weight using the nonparametric reweighting method. 


\begin{equation}
    \hat{weight gain} = (\bar{W}_9-\bar{W}_0)+(0.5)\hat{\beta}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{W}_9$ is the average weight of pregnant women at gestational duration of 9 months or more, and $\bar{W}_0$ is the average prepregnancy weight using the nonparametric reweighting method. To account for additional weight gain between the time of survey and the end of pregnancy for women of gestational duration of 9 months or more, we assume these women are at the midpoint of their last month of pregnancy and add the accordingly scaled $\hat{\beta}$ from the regression in weight gain method 1.

This offers a benchmark to method 1, which may be inaccurate if the assumption is false that women gain 10 times more weight in their second and third trimesters than in their first, or if gestational durations are incorrectly reported.
