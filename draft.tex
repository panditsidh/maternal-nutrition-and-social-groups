\title{Fertility, Household Wealth, and Maternal Nutrition among Indian Social Groups}
\date{\today}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Maternal undernutrition is a threat to women’s health and a leading cause of child mortality and morbidity in India. This paper uses National Family Health Survey 2019-21 data to estimate the fraction of women who are underweight before becoming pregnant -- a key indicator of maternal nutrition -- and presents the first estimates by caste and religious groups.  We use nonparametric reweighting to show that greater than one in five Indian women are underweight before pregnancy.  Adivasi women and Dalit women are 55\%, and 40\%, more likely, respectively, to be underweight before pregnancy than Forward Caste Hindu women and Muslim women. Fertility and birth spacing, which are typically assumed to be related to maternal nutrition, do not explain the gaps we observe.  Instead, differences in household wealth explain a large fraction of the gaps. Notably, and despite multidimensional disadvantage, a similar fraction of Muslim and Forward Caste Hindu women are underweight before pregnancy, and a maternal nutrition advantage among Muslim women appears when we account for the fact that Muslim women live in poorer households. 
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Maternal nutrition is important to women's health, influencing both their pregnancy and postpartum experiences as well as their survival chances.  In undernourished women, limited fat, muscle, and micronutrient reserves together with reduced immune function and blood volume, make it difficult to meet the energy demands of pregnancy.  The inter-generational transmission of health is perhaps nowhere more evident than it is during pregnancy: undernourished mothers are more likely to have low birth weight and preterm babies, who face a higher risk of death than babies born to better nourished mothers \citep{ahmed2013global, aizer2014intergenerational}.  

Prior research has documented a high burden of maternal undernutrition in India, with significant consequences for women's and children's health \citep{bhutta2000has, coffey2015prepregnancy}. Indeed, \cite{fadel2017changes} find that low birth weight and prematurity -- listed as a single cause because they are difficult to disentangle in verbal autopsy data -- is now the leading cause of child mortality in India.  Among surviving children, those born to undernourished mothers are more likely to be malnourished and suffer from chronic illness later in life \citep{underwood2002health, almond2011killing}.  

One population-level indicator of maternal nutrition is the fraction of women who begin pregnancy underweight.  Health guidelines recommend that women who have a body mass index (BMI) of less than 18.5 -- the World Health Organization's cutoff for chronic energy deficiency \citep{who_nlis_malnutrition_women_2021} -- before pregnancy gain 28 to 40 pounds during pregnancy to compensate for poor reserves before pregnancy \citep{yaktine2010weight, ismail2016gestational}.  However, women in low-income settings tend to gain little weight during pregnancy, leaving them and their children at risk of adverse outcomes \citep{coffey2015prepregnancy, wang2020gestational, perumal2023suboptimal}.  

National pregnancy health monitoring systems, such as the birth registration data from the United States Center for Health Statistics, the Danish Medical Birth Registry, and the Birth Registry of Norway, collect self-reported height and prepregnancy weight -- ``[what was] your weight immediately before you became pregnancy with this child?'' -- from women who have recently given birth \citep{cornish2024maternal}.  These data allow researchers to track prepregnancy nutrition and study its relationship with maternal and birth outcomes.  In countries where such data are not collected, researchers may use the proportion of non-pregnant women who are underweight to estimate prepregnancy underweight.  However, this strategy will be flawed if women who become pregnant have different BMIs than women who do not.  

To compute a more accurate estimate of prepregnancy underweight from cross-sectional data, \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy} constructs a non-parametric reweighting function to correct for selection into pregnancy and finds that, in 2005, 42\% of Indian women began pregnancy underweight.  India's rate of prepregnancy underweight was nearly two and half times what it was among sub-Saharan African women in the same period.  

This paper uses the same method to estimate prepregnancy underweight for India in 2019-21 and contributes the first estimates of prepregnancy underweight stratified by Indian social group. It joins a growing literature on health disparities among caste and religious groups in India, where national averages mask stark group-level inequalities \citep{vyas2022social, gupta2020caste, coffey2019local, lopalo2019consequences, geruso2014sanitation, bora2019persistent}. We find that the burden of prepregnancy underweight is highest among Adivasi, or indigenous, groups (28\%), followed by Dalits, a group of oppressed castes who have been treated as ``untouchable'' (25\%).  Muslims, despite social and economic disadvantage, have similar rates of prepregnancy underweight as Hindus from dominant (``forward'') castes (17\% and 18\%, respectively).  

Beyond documenting disparities, this study also investigates explanations.  We first consider whether differences in fertility and birth spacing can account for social group differences in prepregnancy underweight.  We test this hypothesis because prior research on maternal and infant health emphasizes the role of high parity and short birth spacing in ``depleting'' mothers' nutritional status \citep{winkvist1992new}.  Using a Kitagawa decomposition, we find that differences in parity and birth spacing between forward and oppressed caste women account for very little of the gaps that we document.  

Second, we consider whether differences in household wealth can explain the gaps that we observe.  We find that the distributions of women into wealth quartiles explains about 70\% of the forward caste-Adivasi gap, and about 65\% of the forward caste-Dalit gap.  When we apply a Kitawaga decomposition by wealth quartile to the forward caste-Muslim gap, where there was no original gap to be explained, we find that accounting for wealth differences reveals that Muslim women are less likely to be underweight before pregnancy than forward caste women.  This is consistent with research on health disparities that documents health advantages among Muslims \citep{guillot2010hindu, bhalotra2010puzzle, visaria2015preference, geruso2018neighborhood, sharma_gupta_2025}.  We note that although differences in the distribution of women into wealth categories can explain a majority of the gaps we observe, large fractions of the gaps remain unexplained. 

In the next section, we present background information on maternal and child health in India, on Indian social groups and population health, and on the causes of maternal undernutrition.   We then describe our data source -- the National Family Health Survey (NFHS) (2019-21) -- and our methods.  The non-parametric reweighting technique that we apply to the cross-sectional NFHS data corrects for selection into pregnancy.  We apply this technique to the population of Indian women as a whole, and to each social group separately.  Finally, we use Kitagawa decomposition to investigate candidate explanations for group-level disparities in prepregnancy underweight.

\section{Background}

\subsection{Indian social groups and population health}

Caste and religion are important axes of social inequality in India \citep{vaid2014caste, kumar2016explaining, varshney2002ethnic, majid2015indian}. A person's \textit{jati}, or caste sub-group, influences their social interactions, marriage, occupation, and life chances \citep{deshpande2011grammar, thorat2012blocked}. Although caste is a concept closely associated with Hinduism, people from all religious groups in South Asia are stratified along caste lines. 

In an effort to address historical and ongoing discrimination, the central and state governments of India have affirmative action for people from marginalized groups in higher education and government jobs. Affirmative action policies were written into the Indian Constitution, which recognizes three historically disadvantaged groupings of \textit{jatis}. Scheduled Castes (SC) comprise \textit{jatis} that were traditionally considered ``untouchable.''  Both historically and today, Scheduled Castes do stigmatized work, such as work that involves contact with dead bodies (leather work, cremation, removal of dead animals) or feces (cleaning latrines and bathrooms, maintaining sewers, emptying latrine pits).  People from Scheduled Castes refer to themselves as \textit{Dalit}, or ``oppressed people.''  Other Backward Classes (OBC) are \textit{jatis} considered socially, economically, or educationally deprived compared to the dominant, or ``forward,'' castes.  ``Backward'' is a term used in the Constitution; we use the abbreviation ``OBC'' with no normative implications. Scheduled Tribes (ST) refer to India’s indigenous population, who face persistent disadvantage due to geographic isolation and exclusion from state services.  To refer to Scheduled Tribes, we use the term ``Adivasi,'' a term of self-identification that means ``early inhabitants.''  In referring to socially dominant Hindu \textit{jatis}, we use the NFHS' term, ``forward castes.''

Muslims, India's largest religious minority, are about 15\% of the population.  Historically and today, Muslims face social exclusion and targeted violence.  Political movements linking Indian nationalism with Hinduism are central to the marginalization of Muslims \citep{farooqui2020political}. Muslims belong to Dalit, Adivasi, OBC, and forward caste jatis \citep{ahmad2007recognition}, but Muslims are typically not eligible for affirmative action \citep{hasan2018muslims, hasan2022wider}.     

The inequalities created by caste and religion shape population health in India. Dalits, Adivasis, and Muslims have lower life expectancies than forward caste Hindus \citep{vyas2022social, gupta2022large}.  \cite{gupta2020caste} find more self-reported depression and anxiety among Dalits and Adivasis than among forward caste Hindus.  \cite{coffey2019local} document gaps in child height among SC, ST, and OBC children when compared to children from forward castes.

Social marginalization does not always correspond with health disadvantage.  Research finds better health for Muslim children and women on some indicators.  \cite{geruso2018neighborhood} show that Muslim infants are more likely to survive because they are exposed to better neighborhood sanitation, on average, than Hindu infants. \cite{sharma_gupta_2025} find that Muslim women are less likely to experience domestic violence than Hindu women, and that the difference can largely be explained by lower use of alcohol among Muslim men.  \cite{visaria2015preference} finds that selective abortion of girls is lower among Muslims.

\subsection{Maternal and child nutrition in India}

In the late 1990s, researchers noticed that South Asian children had higher rates of malnutrition than children in sub-Saharan Africa, despite being less poor, on average \citep{ramalingaswami1996commentary, underwood2002health}.  They posited that maternal malnutrition, related to the low social status of Indian women, contributed to a high prevalence of low birthweight and widespread child malnurition.  Using the 2005 NFHS, \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy} found that 42\% of Indian women were underweight before pregnancy, and the average woman gained so little weight during pregnancy that she weighed less at the end of pregnancy than the average woman in sub-Saharan Africa weighed at the beginning of pregnancy.  Research has established links between the social status of women within Indian households and their nutrition \citep{coffey2022mothers, hathi2021women}.  

Considering this context, this paper's finding that 22\% of Indian women are underweight before pregnancy indicates significant recent improvement in maternal nutrition in India.  However, even among \textit{forward caste} Hindu women in 2019-2021, prepregnancy underweight remains high compared to other settings.  At 18\%, prepregnancy underweight among forward caste Hindu women is similar to what it was among sub-Saharan African women two decades ago \citep{coffey2015prepregnancy}.  In the United States, fewer than 5\% of women are underweight before pregnancy \citep{zong2022maternal}.  

\subsection{Fertility, household wealth, and nutrition among Indian social groups}

Research on maternal health in low and middle income countries studies ``maternal depletion,'' the hypothesis that higher-order births and low spacing between births leave women nutritionally vulnerable \citep{winkvist1992new, king2003risk, shell2004maternal}.  Can differences in fertility and birth spacing explain differences in prepregnancy underweight across Indian social groups?  Higher fertility among Muslims compared to Hindus has been widely studied \citep{dharmalingam2004pervasive, bhat2005role, james2005accelerated} and has featured prominently in political discourse \citep{jeffery2002population, singh2020numbering}.  Although less well studied, each round of the NFHS finds higher fertility rates among Advisais, Dalits, and OBCs than among forward castes. The literature also notes differences in birth spacing across caste and religious groups, with forward castes having the longest birth spacing \citep{kundu2025short, maity2017comparing, bhalotra2008birth, singh2024wealth}.  

Whether differences in fertility and birth spacing will explain differences in prepregnancy underweight will depend both on the magnitude of fertility and birth spacing differences across groups and on the relationship between parity progression and birth spacing, on the one hand, and nutrition, on the other. \cite{coffey2021neonatal} provide suggestive evidence that, in India, higher parity may not lead to maternal depletion.  They find that, controlling for siblingship size, higher-parity births are more likely to survive the first month of life than lower-parity births.  They posit that this may be because Indian women's social status, and consequently the balance of their food intake and work output, improves as parity and age increase, allowing women to gain weight across successive pregnancies. 

\cite{tagade2020intergroup} estimate that, in 2013, two-thirds of India's wealth was controlled by Hindu forward caste and OBC groups. Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Muslims each had 10\%, 6\%, and 9\% of India's total wealth, respectively, which was considerably less than their population shares of 18\%, 9\%, and 12\%.  There are several pathways through which household wealth may protect against undernutrition: it allows people to buy more and higher quality food \citep{raghunathan2021affordability}, they are more likely to have tools and technology that reduce their energy output \citep{deaton2009food}, and they may be able to afford cleaner water and better sanitation, which reduces energy loss to disease \citep{bhutta2008works}.  Since marginalized castes are more likely to be poor, and to remain poor over time, than forward castes are \citep{thorat2012has, thorat2017escaping}, we expect differences in household wealth to explain at least part of the gaps in prepregnancy underweight across  social groups. 

We note that the relationship between household wealth and women's nutrition is not straightforward.  Calorie consumption in India declined between the 1980s and early 2000s, despite economic growth and reductions in poverty, and despite the fact that Indians were significantly more likely to be underweight than people in other low- and middle-income countries \citep{deaton2009food}.  Data from the 1980s found that, more so than the nutrient intake of men in the same household, women's nutrient intake declined when food prices rose \citep{behrman1990intrahousehold}.  In the 2011 Indian Human Development Survey (IHDS), a quarter of Indian women reported that they are expected to each after the men in their households \citep{hathi2021women}.  

Even if increases in wealth are less likely to translate into improvements in the body mass of young women in India than of young women in settings with less gendered foodways, cross-household differences in wealth may nevertheless partially explain cross-group differences in prepregnancy body mass within India if women in different social groups have similar access to their households' food or rest budgets, or if overall household food budgets matter independently of intrahousehold allocation.  Figure \ref{fig:ihds_eat_last} in the online appendix uses IHDS data to show that the proportion of women who report eating after the men in their households is similar across the five social groups that we study.

\section{Data and Methods}
 
The ideal data for measuring the proportion of women who are underweight before pregnancy would come from a national pregnancy monitoring system.  However, no such representative data exist.  Therefore, we use \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy}'s method of estimating the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight from cross-sectional data.  We construct a nonparametric reweighting function based on observable characteristics of pregnant women, and apply it to representative cross-sectional data on the body mass index scores of non-pregnant women.  

We use the most recent Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) from India, the National Family Health Survey (2019-21) (NFHS 5). These data are publicly available from \texit{www.dhsprogram.com}.  The NFHS 5 is a clustered multistage sample survey that interviewed over 635,000 households and over 720,000 women between the ages of 15 and 49. 

\subsection{Variables}

{\bf{Body mass index.}} Body mass index (BMI) is a person's weight in kilograms divided by her height in meters squared.  The WHO classifies someone as ``underweight'' if her body mass index is less than 18.5.  The NFHS 5 measured women's weights and heights at the time of the survey.  BMI is missing for 2.8\% of pregnant women and 3.4\% of non-pregnant women; these observations are dropped from the analysis.

{\bf{Pregnancy.}}  The NFHS asks women ages 15 to 49 whether they are currently pregnant, and, if so, how many months pregnant they are.  We code a woman as pregnant if she reports being three or more months pregnant.\footnote{If the question on months of pregnancy is not answered, we use reported time since last menstrual period as a measure of gestational duration. Months of pregnancy is missing for only 0.06\% of the pregnant sample.}  We drop women from the analysis who report pregnancies of one or two months duration because not all women are aware of early pregnancies and willing to report them.  

Table \ref{regression3plusmopreg} in the online appendix shows that, among other differences, women who report being one or two months pregnant are more likely to be in the upper half of the wealth distribution than women who report three or more months of pregnancy. Table \ref{gestdur_distribution} in the online appendix shows that a smaller proportion of pregnant women report being one or two months pregnant relative to months three through eight.

{\bf{Social group.}} The NFHS asks respondents whether they belong a Scheduled Caste, a Scheduled Tribe, an Other Backward Class, or a Forward Caste.  It also asks respondents their religion, listing Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, and ``others'' as options. Following the IHDS, we combine these variables into five mutually exclusive, socially meaningful groups \citep{IHDS2005}.  

We code as Adivasi or Dalit those women who report belonging to a Scheduled Tribe or a Scheduled Caste, respectively, regardless of religion.  Women who report their religion as Muslim, and who do not belong to a Scheduled Caste or Tribe, are coded as Muslim. Hindu or Sikh women who report belonging to an Other Backward Class are coded as OBC.  Hindu women who do not belong to a Scheduled Tribe, a Scheduled Caste, or an Other Backward Class are coded as forward caste.  2.03\% of the women interviewed by the NFHS 5 do not fall into any of these categories and are dropped from the analysis. They may identify as Christians, Jains, or as not having a religious identity.

{\bf{Variables used in nonparametric reweighting.}} Our method for estimating prepregnancy underweight, described below, involves matching non-pregnant to pregnant women on variables that correct for selection into pregnancy.  We construct the following variables. When constructed for pregnant women, variables are coded according to what was true when the woman became pregnant.  When constructed for non-pregnant, they are constructed according to what was true at the time of the survey.

\begin{itemize}
\item {\bf{not using modern contraception:}} A binary indicator that the woman is not using a modern method of contraception.  The information is obtained from the reproductive calendar.  Modern contraception is defined as the pill, an IUD, injectables, a diaphragm, condoms, or male or female sterilization.\footnote{Perhaps surprisingly, 4.56\% of pregnant women report having used a method of modern contraception in the month before they became pregnant.} 
\item {\bf{less than a primary education:}} A binary indicator for not having finished primary school.
\item {\bf{rural resident:}} A respondent is a rural resident if she was interviewed in a rural primary sampling unit. 
\item {\bf{does not have a boy child:}} A binary indicator that a woman does not have any sons.
\item {\bf{age group:}} 15-19, 20-24, 25-29, 30-49 years old
\item {\bf{parity and birthspacing:}} A categorical variable that captures both parity and birth spacing. Women who are pregnant with their first child are coded as parity 0, as are non-pregnant women who have no children.  For pregnant women, birth spacing is measured from the month of the previous live birth to the end of the current pregnancy, assuming that the current pregnancy will last nine months. For non-pregnant women, spacing is measured from the month of the previous live birth to nine months after the date of the interview. 
We construct the following categories: (1) Parity 0; (2) Parity 1, below 2 years spacing; (3) Parity 1, 2–3 years spacing; (4) Parity 1, 3+ years spacing; (5) Parity 2, 2 years spacing; (6) Parity 2, 2–3 years spacing; (7) Parity 2, 3+ years spacing; (8) Parity 3+, below 2 years spacing; (9) Parity 3+, 2–3 years spacing; and (10) Parity 3+, 3+ years spacing.
\item {\bf{wealth quartile:}} The NFHS publishes a wealth index score for each household based on a principal component analysis of whether or not the household owns set of assets (for example, a bicycle, a television, or a car), and housing characteristics (for example, source of drinking water, toilet facilities, and flooring materials) \citep{iips_icf_nfhs5_2022}. We split the wealth index score into quartiles. 
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Estimation of prepregnancy nutrition}

We apply a nonparametric reweighting function to the body mass index scores of nonpregnant women to make their distribution of the observable characteristics match that of pregnant women. This counterfactual distribution up-weights nonpregnant women who are more likely to become pregnant and down-weights those who are less likely to become pregnant, producing estimates that more closely approximate nutritional status at the beginning of pregnancy. This method addresses the challenge that we cannot directly observe the nutrition status of women at the time of conception. 
 
The reweighting function \(\psi\) is defined as \(\psi(x)=[f(x|P=1)]/[f(x|P=0)]\), the ratio of the weighted counts of pregnant to nonpregnant women in each bin \(x\), where a ``bin'' is a unique combination of the predictors.  The reweighting function is used to estimate average BMI after being multiplied by each woman's sampling weight: \(\tilde{W}_i = W_i * \psi(x_i)\), where nonpregnant woman \(i\) in bin \(x_i\) has a sampling weight \(\tilde{W}_i\). 

There are 2,560 unique bins, though not all of them contain both pregnant and nonpregnant women.  Bins that contain nonpregnant women, but not pregnant women, do not pose a problem for the analysis; they receive a weight of zero.  However, bins that contain pregnant women, but no nonpregnant women, must be dropped from the analysis. Table \ref{tab:sampledropped} in the online appendix shows the sample sizes of pregnant women for each of the subgroup analyses, and the percentage of pregnant women dropped for each subgroup because they are in a bin that contains no nonpregnant women.  

The reweighting procedure was conducted for the five social groups together, and within each social group.  When we decompose differences in the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight by social group, we compute estimates of prepregnancy underweight within each social group's parity and birth spacing bins, and within each social group's wealth quartile bins, for a total of 76 estimates of the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight.  

Standard errors are constructed using cluster bootstrapping.  We draw 30,131 clusters\footnote{This is slightly less than the number of primary sampling units in the NFHS 5 because we drop women from the analysis who do not belong to one of the social groups we study.} with replacement and compute the proportion of prepregnancy underweight in that sample, and repeat the procedure 1,000 times.  We present confidence intervals spanning the 5th and 95th percentiles of the distribution of resulting proportions of prepregnancy underweight.

\subsection{Kitagawa decomposition of differences in prepregnancy underweight between marginalized groups and forward castes}

We conduct two Kitagawa decompositions \citep{kitagawa1955components}: the first decomposes gaps between marginalized and dominant groups by the parity and birth spacing variable, and the second decomposes gaps by wealth quartile.  The overall gap in the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight between a marginalized group and forward caste Hindus can be expressed as the sum of differences ``explained'' by the variable of interest and differences that remain ``unexplained:''

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kitagawa}
\Delta \equiv \bar Y^{g} - \bar Y^{f}
= \Delta_{explained} + \Delta_{unexplained}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation} \label{eq_explained}
    \Delta_{explained} = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \big(p_j^{g} - p_j^{f}\big)\,\frac{Y_j^{g} + Y_j^{f}}{2}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation} \label{eq_unexplained}
    \Delta_{unexplained} = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \big(Y_j^{g} - Y_j^{f}\big)\,\frac{p_j^{g} + p_j^{f}}{2}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item where \(j=1,...,J\) indexes categories of the decomposition variable (for parity and birth spacing J=10, for wealth quartiles J=4)
    \item \( \bar Y^g\) and \(\bar Y^f\) are the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight for groups \(g\) (marginalized group) and \(f\) (forward caste Hindu)
    \item \(Y_j^g\) and \(Y_j^f\) are the means of prepregnancy underweight within category \(j\) of groups \(g\) and \(f\) respectively
    \item \(p_j^g\) and \(p_j^f\) are the proportions of women in each category \(j\) of the variable of interest
    
\end{itemize}

The percent of the gap explained by the variable of interest is computed as:

\begin{equation} \label{eq_pct}
    \frac{\Delta_{explained}}{\Delta_{total}}.
\end{equation}

\section{Results}

\subsection*{Descriptive statistics}

Table \ref{tab:sumstats} shows sample means for the variables we use in the reweighting decomposition, separately by social group and pregnancy status. Pregnant and nonpregnant women have different characteristics, on average. Pregnant women are younger and at lower parities: 43\% are aged 20–24 (versus 16\% of nonpregnant women), and 42\% have no prior births (versus 30\% of nonpregnant women). This highlights the potential biases of estimating prepregnancy BMI from the BMIs of nonpregnant women without adjustment for these covariates.

There are meaningful differences across social groups in these predictors of pregnancy. Nearly half of nonpregnant Adivasi women have less than primary education, compared to one in five nonpregnant forward caste women. Nearly half of nonpregnant Adivasi women and more than a quarter of nonpregnant Dalit women, compared with only 10\% of nonpregnant forward caste women have household wealth in the first (bottom) quartile. Adivasi and Dalit women are also overrepresented in the first quartile of household wealth among pregnant women.

To further demonstrate the value of adjusting for these covariates in the non-parametric reweighting analysis, Table \ref{tab:regression} in the online appendix shows the results of OLS linear probability models that regress pregnancy on each of the covariates.  The  models are estimated separately for each social group, and for all five groups combined. When looking at all social groups combined, each covariate, with the exception of being at parity 2 with 2-3 years of birth spacing, predicts pregnancy with statistical significance.

\subsection*{Prepregnancy underweight by social group}

Figure \ref{fig:mainresult} shows estimates of the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight by social group, and for all five groups combined. With the exception of forward caste Hindus and Muslims, confidence intervals do not overlap for any two social groups, indicating statistically significant differences in the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight at the 95\% level. The burden of prepregnancy underweight is highest among Adivasi women: 28\% are underweight at the onset of pregnancy. Dalit and OBC prepregnant women are also at higher risk, with 25\% and 23\% underweight before pregnancy, respectively. The prevalence of prepregnancy underweight is lowest, although nevertheless high in international comparison, among the forward caste Hindus (18\%) and Muslims (17\%). Combining all five social groups, we find that 22\% of prepregnant women in India are underweight. Table \ref{specifications} in the online appendix shows that the results are robust to changes in the variables used in the reweighting procedure.  

\subsection*{Social group differences in parity, birth spacing, and household wealth}

In order to motivate the Kitagawa decompositions, panels A, C, and E of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} show the distributions of pregnant women, by social group, into parity, birth spacing, and household wealth categories.  Panels B, D, and F of \ref{fig:sixpanel} show estimates of the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight, for all social groups combined, within each parity/birth spacing and household wealth category.  

\subsubsection*{Parity}

Panel A of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} shows that forward caste Hindu pregnant women are more likely than pregnant women from other social groups to be pregnant women with their first child (parity 0).   Almost half of pregnant forward caste Hindu women are at parity 0, compared to 38-39\% of Muslim and Adivasi pregnant women. Muslim pregnant women are more likely than women from other social groups to be at higher parities, with 16\% at parity 3 or higher compared to 5\% among forward caste Hindu women and 8\% among OBC women. Although present, fertility differences across social groups are not very large.  Table \ref{tab:tfr} in the online appendix complements the information in Panel A of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} by showing total fertility rates (TFR) for each of the five social groups based on births in the three years prior to the NFHS 5.  TFR for forward caste Hindu women is 1.6, compared to 2.3 among Muslims, and 2.1 among Adivasis and Dalits.  Fertility in India today is very close to replacement; higher order births are becoming rare.

Panel B of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} shows that the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight is highest among women who have no prior births.  One in four women who have no prior births are estimated to be underweight at the start of pregnancy, compared to about one in five women at parities one, two, and three plus.  The cross-sectional nature of the data limits what we can conclude about the relationship between parity \textit{progression} and maternal nutrition.  Poorer women are more likely to be undernourished at any parity, and are more likely to progress to higher order births than wealthier women.  Therefore, it is consistent with the pattern observed in panel B that, as women progress through their childbearing careers, they gain weight in successive pregnancies, on average, and are less likely to be underweight before higher order pregnancies.

\subsubsection*{Birth spacing}

Panel C of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} summarizes birth spacing for the 14,304 pregnant women in the NFHS 5 who had at least one prior birth.  Compared to women from other social groups, Dalit women were most likely to have birth spacing below two years.  28\% of Dalit pregnant women, compared to about 20\% of forward caste Hindu, Adivasi, and Muslim pregnant women, are expected to have their child within two years of a prior birth.  Three or more years of birth spacing is the modal birth spacing for all social groups.  Fifty-five percent of Muslim pregnant women are expected to have at least three years birth spacing, compared to 40\% of Dalit pregnant women. Panel D of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} shows that the estimated prevalence of prepregnancy underweight varies little by birthspacing.

\subsubsection*{Household Wealth}

Panel E of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} shows stark differences in the distribution of household wealth across Indian social groups. Half of Adivasi pregnant women live in households in the lowest wealth quartile, compared to only 12\% of forward caste Hindu pregnant women. By contrast, nearly 44\% of forward caste Hindu pregnant women are in the top quartile, compared with fewer than one in five Dalit or Adivasi pregnant women. Panel F of Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel}estimates prepregnancy underweight by household wealth category.  In contrast to panels B and D, which show the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight varies little by parity or by birth spacing, a clear and quantitatively important pattern emerges in panel F.  The prevalence of prepregnancy underweight is inversely related to household wealth.  30\% of women in the poorest quartile are estimated to be underweight at the start of pregnancy, compared to 13\% in the top quartile.  

\subsection*{Decompositions of differences in prepregnancy underweight across social groups}\label{sec:kitagawa}

Table \ref{decomposition} summarizes Kitagawa decompositions of the gaps in the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight between women from marginalized groups and forward caste Hindu women.  The top row of Table \ref{decomposition} shows the raw gaps from Figure \ref{fig:mainresult}, the largest of which is the Adivasi-Forward gap of 10.17 percentage points.

Panel A shows that the combined parity and birthspacing variable explains little to none of the forward caste advantage in prepregnancy underweight relative to Adivasi, Dalit, and OBC women (-1\% to 5\% of the gaps).  We note that there was no statistically significant gap to be explained between Muslim and forward caste Hindu women.

Differences in the distributions of household wealth, in contrast, explain a large portion of the forward caste Hindu advantage in prepregnancy underweight relative to Adivasis, Dalits, and OBCs: 73\%, 65\%, and 52\% of the respective gaps.  The decomposition of the gap between Muslims and forward castes suggests that if Muslims had the same distribution of wealth as forward caste Hindus, they might face a meaningfully lower burden of prepregnancy underweight than forward caste Hindus. 


\section{Discussion}

This paper updates the literature on maternal undernutrition in India and contributes the first estimates the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight by social group. Notably, in 2019-21, more than one in five women in India was underweight at the beginning of pregnancy, resulting in risks to her own health and to her child's health.  On one hand, the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight has come down by 50\% since 2005.  On the other, India's 2021 prevalence of prepregnancy underweight is still greater than sub-Saharan Africa's in the early 2000s.  According to World Economic Outlook data from the International Monetary Fund, Gross Domestic Product per capita was 50\% greater in India in 2020 than it was in sub-Saharan Africa in 2005 \cite{IMF2025GDPperCapita}.

Within India, the burden of maternal undernutrition falls disproportionately on Adivasi, Dalit, and OBC women.  Although not the focus of this paper, we note that these groups do not compensate for high prevalences of prepregnancy underweight with higher weight gain during pregnancy.  Table \ref{tab:addnut} in the online appendix shows two different estimates of weight gain during pregnancy for each social group.\footnote{The methods for deriving these two estimates of weight gain during pregnancy from cross-sectional data were developed by \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy} and are detailed in section \ref{sec:weightgain} of the online appendix.}$^{,}$\footnote{Table \ref{tab:addnut} also computes the prevalence of prepregnancy obesity ($BMI>30$) in India and by social group, using the same method we used to compute the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight.  We include these estimates for completeness and to encourage future research on maternal nutrition, broadly defined.  Although obesity rates are increasing in India \citep{luhar2020forecasting}, obesity among women who are likely to become pregnant is relatively uncommon: 8\% of Indian women are estimated to be obese before pregnancy.  There are small disparities by social group: 11\% of forward caste Hindu and Muslim women are obese before pregnancy, compared to 5\% of Adivasi women, 7\% of Dalit women, and 8\% of OBC women.} Weight gain during pregnancy is not statistically significantly different across social groups.  For all groups and all estimates, it is lower than the 28 to 40 pounds that is recommended by the United States' Institute of Medicine (IOM) guidelines \citep{iom2009weightgain}. \footnote{We cite the IOM guidelines because the Government of India has not issued guidelines on weight gain in pregnancy. The IOM guidelines have also been endorsed by the United Kingdom's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence \citep{NICE2025}.}

We note the similarity in the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight between forward caste Hindu and Muslim women.  Despite social and economic disadvantage, Muslim women are no more likely to be underweight before pregnancy, and are less likely to be underweight after accounting for household wealth.  This result coheres with other research that finds advantages in Muslim women and children's health relative to Hindus'.  Could this be because Muslim households allocate food more equally among young women and other family members than Hindus?  Using the 2005 IHDS, \cite{desai2014muslim} found that Muslim women were nine percentage points less likely to report eating after the men in their household than Hindu women.  Our analysis of the 2011 IHDS finds that the prevalence of the practice decreased across the two survey waves from TK, and that the difference between Hindus and Muslims is no longer statistically significant \citep{IHDS2005, IHDS2011}.  To our knowledge, no study or dataset allows a more nuanced picture of how gendered foodways differ across Indian social groups.  Future research could investigate whether childbearing aged women eat after other women in the household, and whether they are discouraged from eating higher-calorie foods, such as meat, dairy products, and fried foods, even when they are available for others.\footnote{\cite{harris2017determinants} provides a comprehensive review of the literature on intrahousehold allocation of food among South Asian adults.  They note these and other discriminatory food practices faced by women in South Asia.} 

The Kitagawa decompositions presented in section \ref{sec:kitagawa} show that higher fertility and more closely spaced births among Adivasis, Dalits, and OBCs do not explain why they are more likely to be underweight before pregnancy than forward caste Hindu women.  This is in part because the differences by social group in parity and birth spacing are small, and in part because these variables have little correlation with prepregnancy underweight in the most recent Indian data. 

Differences in household wealth, in contrast, explain large proportions of the gaps.  Future studies might investigate whether this is because wealthier households are able to afford more and better food, because wealthier households can afford labor or energy saving devices such as vehicles or washing machines, or because wealthier households can better protect young women against disease.  There is some evidence that women may cut back on food consumption during times of scarcity in order to ensure that men have the energy to work \citep{behrman1990intrahousehold}.  It is reasonable to assume that times of scarcity are more common in poorer households than richer ones.  If so, discriminatory food practices could be more common in poor households than richer ones.  However, there has been substantial economic growth since this study was conducted, and a substantial reduction in the proportion of households reporting food insecurity \citep{TK}.  Further, \cite{harris2017determinants} suggest that oppressed caste households may be more egalitarian in food allocation that forward caste households.  This topic merits further study; possible reasons for more egalitarian foodways in oppressed caste households could be that oppressed caste women are more likely to work outside the home -- and therefore are perceived to need energy for income generation -- and that oppressed caste women are less likely than forward caste Hindu women to fast on a regular basis.  

We also note that a significant fraction of the gaps in the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight remains unexplained after accounting for differences in household wealth.  We expect that if we were able to measure differences in stress related to discrimination, that could explain at least part of the remaining gaps.  This is an important hypothesis to consider, as the literature on racial disparities in birth outcomes in the United States increasingly points to stress related to racism as an important reason for higher rates of preterm birth and low birth weight among Black infants \citep{hogue2005stress, braveman2015role, sosnaud2021cross}. Future research should aim to unpack not only how and at what cost childbearing-age women experience discrimination based on social group membership, but also how food allocation to young women may vary across social groups.  

Can policies reduce maternal undernutrition and address disparities in maternal nutrition?  Policies to reduce prepregnancy underweight, in particular, may be more effective if they focus on unmarried women.  The average amount of time between marriage and conception is short: on average, women who had their first births in the three years before the NFHS 5 were married for only 17.23 months before conception.  \cite{banerjee2024persistent} study a cash transfer program in West Bengal in which girls between the ages of 13 and 18 received Rs. 1,000 for every year they remained unmarried and in school.  They received Rs. 25,000 if they were still unmarried and in school when they turned 18.  The authors did not study the impacts of the program on young women's nutrition, but found that women who were eligible for the program married later and and were less likely to say that domestic violence was justified than those who did not participate in the program.  Research could usefully study whether similar or larger transfers, perhaps delivered with messages about the risks of remaining underweight, would improve prepregnancy nutrition.  Considering the differences across social groups in the marginal benefit of a fixed amount of cash, whether such a program were universal or targeted to oppressed caste women, it would likely improve equity.  

Our research also underscores the value of cash transfers to pregnant women, which were made an entitlement of no less than Rs. 6,000 by the 2013 National Food Security Act.  However, \cite{dreze2021derailed} find that the government has allocated only a fraction of the funds to which pregnant women were entitled since 2013: in 2020, allocation to maternity entitlements was only enough to cover about 1 in 7 women who gave birth.  When they are disbursed, maternity entitlements are often restricted to the first child and are conditional on the pregnant woman receiving prenatal care from government health workers \citep{kalra2020birth}.  If fully funded, indexed for inflation, given at the start of pregnancy, and linked, in public discourse, to the need for increasing weight gain in pregnancy, cash transfers during pregnancy would be more likely to help prevent the adverse consequences of India's high prevalence of prepregnancy underweight \citep{coffey2016underweight}. Policymakers interested in addressing disparities in nutrition during pregnancy might also consider increasing the amount of the transfer to women from Dalit, Adivasi, and OBC backgrounds.

\section{S.\arabic{section} Measuring weight gain during pregnancy} \label{sec:weightgain}

\subsection{S.\arabic{section}.\arabic{subsection} Weight gain method 1} \label{sec:weightgainm1}

The first method of estimating weight gain starts by estimating monthly weight gain of pregnant women at 3+ months gestational duration as $\hat{\beta}$ from an OLS regression of the form
\begin{equation}
    weight_i = \alpha + \beta month_i + \tau C_i + \epsilon_i
\end{equation}

where $weight_i$ is the weight in kilograms of pregnant woman $i$. $month_i$ is month of gestation, calculated as described in the methods section, and $C_i$ are controls, including women's current ages (single year), years of education, number of living children, urban or rural residence, wealth quintile, as well as state-month fixed effects.

Assuming women gain 10 times more weight in their second and third trimesters than in their first, we estimate weight gain for a full-term pregnancy as 
\begin{equation}
    \hat{weight gain} = 1.1\times6\times\hat{\beta}
\end{equation}

In table A1, the 95\% confidence intervals for weight gain method 1 are calculated as $1.1\times6\times\hat{\beta} \pm 1.96\times se_{\hat{\beta}}$.

\subsection{S.\arabic{section}.\arabic{subsection} Weight gain method 2} \label{sec:weightgainm2}


The second method of estimating weight gain takes the difference of the average weight of pregnant women at gestational duration of 9 months or more and the average prepregnancy weight using the nonparametric reweighting method. 


\begin{equation}
    \hat{weight gain} = (\bar{W}_9-\bar{W}_0)+(0.5)\hat{\beta}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{W}_9$ is the average weight of pregnant women at gestational duration of 9 months or more, and $\bar{W}_0$ is the average prepregnancy weight using the nonparametric reweighting method. To account for additional weight gain between the time of survey and the end of pregnancy for women of gestational duration of 9 months or more, we assume these women are at the midpoint of their last month of pregnancy and add the accordingly scaled $\hat{\beta}$ from the regression in weight gain method 1.

This offers a benchmark to method 1, which may be inaccurate if the assumption is false that women gain 10 times more weight in their second and third trimesters than in their first, or if gestational durations are incorrectly reported.
