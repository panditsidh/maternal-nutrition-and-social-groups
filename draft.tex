\title{Fertility and Maternal Nutrition by Indian Social Group}
\author{authors}
\date{\today}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Maternal undernutrition remains an important threat to women's health and an important cause of child mortality and morbidity worldwide. Prior research shows that maternal undernutrition is particularly high in India. This paper estimates prepregnancy underweight using India's National Family Health Survey (2019-21) (NFHS-5), and compares prepregnancy underweight across social groups.  We use nonparametric reweighting to show that the burden of prepregnancy underweight in India has reduced in recent decades -- from about 42\% in 2005 to about 22\% in 2019-21.  We find that prepregnancy underweight is about 55\% higher among Adivasis and 40\% higher among Dalits than it is among Forward Caste Hindus and Muslims.  We find that fertility and birth spacing, factors typically assumed to contribute to maternal undernutrition, do not explain differences in prepregnancy underweight across Indian social groups. Instead, accounting for differences in economic status explains much of the prepregnancy underweight gap between Adivasis and Forward Castes, and between Dalits and Forward Castes. Accounting for wealth differences reveals that despite multidimensional disadvantage, Muslims have lower rates of prepregnancy underweight than Forward Caste Hindus.  
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Maternal nutrition is important to women's health, influencing both their lived experiences and their survival chances.  In undernourished women, limited fat, muscle, and micronutrient reserves combine with reduced immune function and blood volume to make it difficult to meet the energy demands of pregnancy, increasing the risk of complications for both mother and child.  The inter-generational transmission of health is perhaps nowhere more evident than it is during pregnancy: undernourished mothers are more likely to have low birth weight and preterm babies, who face a higher risk of death than babies born to better nourished mothers \citep{ahmed2013global}.  Among surviving children, those born to undernourished mothers are more likely to be malnourished themselves.

India has historically had a high burden of maternal under =nutrition, with significant consequences for their own and for child health. \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy} found that, in 2005, 42\% of Indian women began pregnancy with a body mass index less than 18.5, the World Health Organization cutoff for ``chronic energy deficiency.''  This was nearly two and half times the rate of prepregnancy underweight among women in sub-Saharan Africa, where 17\% of prepregnant women were underweight in the early 2000s.  According to \cite{ahmed2013global}, maternal undernutrition is a major cause of intrauterine growth restriction in South Asia. Using data from the Sample Registration System and the Million Deaths study, \cite{fadel2017changes} find that low birth weight and prematurity (listed as a single cause because they are difficult to disentangle in verbal autopsy) is now the leading cause of child mortality in India.  

This paper presents updated estimates of prepregnancy underweight in India and contributes the first estimates of prepregnancy underweight by social group. It joins a growing literature on health disparities among caste and religious groups in India, where national averages mask stark group-level inequalities \citep{vyas2022social, gupta2020caste, coffey2019local, lopalo2019consequences, geruso2014sanitation}. We find that about 22\% of Indian women have BMIs of less than 18.5 before pregnancy.  28\% of Adivasis (also called India's ``indigenous'' or ``tribal'' groups), 25\% of Dalits (also called Scheduled Castes or ``former untouchables''), and 23\% of Other Backward Classes (an official term used by the Government of India for oppressed castes that have not been considered ``untouchable''), compared to 18\% of forward-caste Hindus and 17\% of Muslims, are underweight before pregnancy.  Rates of prepregnancy underweight are therefore 55\% higher among Adivasis than among forward castes and Muslims, and 40\% higher among Dalits. 

This study investigates the reasons for these disparities.  We first consider whether differences in fertility and birth spacing can account for social group differences in prepregnancy underweight.  We test this hypothesis because prior research on maternal and infant health emphasizes the role of high parity and short birth spacing in ``depleting'' mothers' nutritional status \citep{winkvist1992new}.  Using a Kitagawa decomposition, we find that differences in parity and birth spacing between forward and oppressed caste women account for very little (in no individual comparison more than 5\%) of the disparities we document.  

Second, we consider whether differences in economic resources can explain the gaps that we observe.  We find that the distributions of prepregnant women into wealth quartiles can explain about 70\% of the forward caste-Adivasi gap, and about 65\% of the forward caste-Dalit gap.  When we apply a Kitawaga decomposition by wealth category to the forward caste-Muslim gap, where there was no original gap to be explained, the analysis suggests that after accounting for wealth differences, Muslim women are less likely to be underweight before pregnancy than forward caste women.  This is consistent with other papers on health disparities that document some health advantages among Muslims \cite{geruso2018neighborhood}.  We note that although differences in the distribution of women into wealth categories can explain a majority of the gaps we observe, large fractions of the gaps remain unexplained. 

In the next section, we present background information on Indian social groups and population, on maternal health in India, and on cause of maternal undernutrition.   We then describe our data source -- the National Family Health Survey (2019-21) -- and our methods.  In brief, we compute prepregnancy underweight using a non-parametric reweighting technique to identify a group of nonpregnant women who are especially likely to become pregnant in the near future.  We apply this technique to the population of Indian women as a whole, and to each social group separately.  Finally, we investigate candidate explanations for group-level disparities in maternal underweight.

\section{Background}

\subsection{Maternal and child health in India}

In the late 1990s and early 2000s, researchers noticed that South Asian children had higher rates of malnutrition than children in sub-Saharan Africa, despite being less poor \citep{ramalingaswami1996commentary, underwood2002health}.  They posited that poor maternal nutrition, related to the low social status of Indian women, was a reason for widespread child malnurition.  Using Demographic and Health Surveys from the early 2000s, \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy} found that 42\% of Indian women were underweight before pregnancy, and the average woman gained so little weight during pregnancy that she weighed less at the end of pregnancy than the average woman in sub-Saharan Africa weighed at the beginning of pregnancy.  Research also found undernutrition to be more common among Indian women with lower intrahousehold status, with implications for the health and survival of their children \citep{coffey2022mothers}.  

Although this paper does not contribute updated comparisons of maternal nutrition across low income countries, nor does it contribute updated estimates of maternal nutrition by intrahoushold status, this background information contextualizes the maternal nutrition disparities across social groups presented in this paper.  The rate of pre-pregnancy underweight that we compute for forward caste women (18\%) is high compared to rich countries -- in the Uninted States fewer than 5\% of women are underweight before pregnancy \citep{zong2022maternal} -- and is similar to the rate of prepregnancy underweight among sub-Saharan African women two decades ago \citep{coffey2015prepregnancy}.  Yet, this paper's finding that 22\% of Indian women are underweight before pregnancy also represents rapid improvement in maternal nutrition in recent years.  This improvement, combined with a rapid increase in the proportion of births that occur in medical facilities rather than at home, has certainly contributed to improvements in neonatal mortality.

\subsection{Indian social groups and population health}

Caste and religion are important axes of social inequality in India \citep{vaid2014caste}. Codified in ancient Hindu texts, the caste system divides people into four hereditary, hierarchical social groups, or \textit{varnas}. In day to day life, however, a person's \textit{jati}, or caste sub-group, is the more relevant social identity. \textit{Jatis} influence patterns of marriage, social interaction, occupations, and life chances \citep{deshpande2011grammar, thorat2012blocked}. Although caste is a concept most closely associated with Hinduism, people from all religious groups are stratified along caste lines. 

In an effort to address historical and ongoing discrimination, the central and state governments of India have affirmative action in educational institutions and government jobs for people from marginalized \textit{jatis}. Affirmative action policies were written into the Indian Constitution; it recognizes three historically disadvantaged groups as eligible for affirmative action. First, Scheduled Castes comprise \textit{jatis} that were traditionally considered ``untouchable.''  Both historically and today, people from these groups do stigmatized work such as leather work, cremation, and the removal of human feces.  People from Scheduled Castes refer to themselves as \texit{Dalit}, or ``oppressed people.''  We use this term in our paper.  Second, Other Backward Classes (OBC) are \textit{jatis} considered socially, economically, or educationally deprived compared to the dominant, or ``forward,'' castes.  ``Backward'' is a term used in the Indian Constitution and subsequent policies; we use the abbreviation ``OBC'' throughout the paper with no normative implications. Finally, Scheduled Tribes (ST) refers to India’s indigenous population, who face persistent disadvantage due to geographic isolation and exclusion from state services.  To refer to people from Scheduled Tribes, we use the term ``Adivasi,'' a term of self-identification that means ``early inhabitants.''  To refer to people from historically dominant \textit{jatis} who are ineligible for affirmative action, we use the term ``forward castes.'' This is the term for dominant castes used by our data source, National Family Health Survey.

Muslims comprise 14\% of India's population.  Historically and today, Muslims face social exclusion and targeted violence.  Political movements linking Indian nationalism with Hinduism are central to the marginalization of Muslims \citep{farooqui2020political}. Muslims are among Dalit, Adivasi, OBC, and forward caste categories, though many Muslims belong to traditionally artisan and peasant castes \citep{ahmad2007recognition}.   Those belonging to one of the three oppressed groups face unique and multidimensional disadvantage.  

The inequalities created by the institutions of caste and religion shape population health in India.  For example, \cite{gupta2022large} find that Dalits and Adivasis have life expectancies between 4 and 7 years lower than forward caste Hindus.  Muslim life expectancy is about 3 years less than that of forward caste Hindus.  \cite{gupta2020caste} find higher prevalences of self-reported depression and anxiety among Dalits and Adivasis than among forward caste Hindus.  \cite{coffey2019local} document gaps in child height among children from SC, OBC, ST, and forward caste backgrounds.

Social marginalization does not always correspond with health disadvantage; some research documents Muslims health advantages. \cite{geruso2018neighborhood} find that Muslim infants are more likely to survive because they tend to live in Muslim dominated neighborhoods which have better sanitation than Hindu-dominated neighborhoods.  Gupta and Sharma find that Muslim women are less likely to experience domestic violence than Hindu women, and that the difference can largely be explained by lower use of alcohol among Muslim men.

\subsection{Caste, religion, fertility, and maternal health}

Research investigating social group disparities in maternal health in India tends to focus on differences in the use of health care. For example, \cite{dommaraju2008pervasive}, \cite{kumar2016explaining}, \cite{das2022caste} use NFHS 1998-99, NFHS 2005, and NFHS 2014-15 data, respectively, to document that SC and ST mothers are less likely to get prenatal and delivery care than forward caste mothers.  Muslims women get less prenatal care than Hindus, but deliver in similar types of hospitals at similar rates \citep{bango2024pattern, ohlan2020muslim}. We know of no other study that documents social group differences in maternal anthropometry.

Higher fertility among Muslims relative to Hindus has been widely studied by demographers \citep{dharmalingam2004pervasive, bhat2005role, james2005accelerated} and has played a significant role in political debates in India \citep{jeffery2002population}.  At least since the first National Family Health Survey in 1992, women from oppressed castes have had higher fertility rates than women from forward castes, though these patterns have been less well studied.  The prior literature also notes longer birth spacing for forward caste women \.  When considering the reasons for social group level differences in maternal health, we consider what role parity and birth spacing differences might play.  Because fetal growth and lactation draw heavily on maternal nutrient stores, researchers have found that higher-order births, especially those that have low spacing from the prior birth, can leave women nutritionally depleted \citep{winkvist1992new, king2003risk}.

However, it is unclear how much of India's poor maternal nutrition can be attributed to maternal depletion, and whether it contributes to social group disparities in maternal nutrition. \cite{kundu2025short} finds that Indian women with birth intervals of less than 33 months were 1.39 times more likely to be underweight, and more likley to have had a birth that resulted in ab infant or child death. However, women who have higher parity births and low birth spacing -- and who are therefore at risk of maternal depletion -- are also more likely to have lower socioeconomic status.  Low SES can cause underweight through other pathways, such as inadequate diets, disease exposure, and heavy physical workloads. Thus, it is difficult to disentangle the specific contribution of parity and birthspacing to the prevalence of underweight from the broader socioeconomic factors that place women at risk of undernutrition.


The other dimension of maternal depletion is parity, which has been affected by the fertility transition in India at differing rates across social groups. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \cite{srinivas2024rapid}: fertility gap between Hindus and Muslims dropped from 1.1 to 0.42. SC, ST and OBC fertility decline is converging fast  - levels are well below replacement. the wanted number of births per woman is even more convergent - so differences are likely due to unmet need for contraception. so effective family planning could accelerate convergence. also differences in fertility rates between caste groups are much smaller than difference in fertility rates by education and economic status within caste group - fertility is mainly driven by other socio-economic and regional factors and effective health policies.
\end{itemize}

Slower fertility decline in disadvantaged social groups can increase relative exposure to maternal depletion holding birth spacing constant, thus widening gaps in prepregnancy health. On the other hand, overall convergence in fertility rates across social groups may reduce gaps, and the overall exposure of maternal depletion. However, the actual relationship between parity and maternal and child health outcomes in India is counterintuitive to the maternal depletion hypothesis. \cite{coffey2021neonatal} finds that Indian mothers are actually less likely to be underweight (and give birth to a child that dies as a neonate) at later parities. This may be due to well observed relationship between childbearing and women's intrahousehold status. Therefore, it's unclear how big of a role maternal depletion plays in creating prepregnancy health social group inequalities as fertility declines in India.


However, the causes of poor health among marginalized groups in India are multidimensional, and the relative contribution of factors is not well understood. Wealth is an important determinant of health because it enables public investment in health infrastructure and sufficient expenditure on diet and medicine. Additionally, low incomes can generate stress that both directly damages health and increases associated unhealthy behaviors. Wealth also improves literacy and education access, which enable better engagement with health infrastructure and individual protection of health \citep{rajan2013wealthier}. 

Indeed, marginalized groups in India have much lower wealth, likely by virtue of these systems of discrimination. Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes have household incomes up to 30\% less than the national average, and OBCs and Muslims own up to 10\% less \citep{bharti2018wealth}.

Despite these stark gaps, studies that conduct decompositions on social group inequalities in health in India find mixed results on the predictive power of socioeconomic status. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \cite{bora2019persistent}: 76\% of U5MR gap between SC/ST and non SC/ST is explained - mostly by women’s education and household wealth, but also place of birth and number of antenatal care (ANC) visit

    \item \cite{gupta2020caste}: caste and religion gaps in mental health were not explained by SES

    \item \cite{vyas2022social}: Economic status explains less than half of the gaps in life expectancy across social groups

    \item \cite{dutta2020effect}: Caste had an independent effect on anaemia and sickness absenteeism in school children, unexplained by inter-caste economic disparities.
\end{itemize}

The large components of health inequalities unexplained by wealth highlight the importance of non-economic mechanisms - such as exposure to health risks, discrimination, and health behaviors - in shaping health outcomes. Poor health can often cut across socioeconomic lines in unexpected ways.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \cite{geruso2018neighborhood}: Muslim children are less likely to die in infancy despite socioeconomic disadvantage

    \item \cite{srivastav2023understanding}: private childbirth facilities that serve wealthier mothers have higher neonatal mortality 
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
 

Despite the urgency, India has no national pregnancy monitoring system for accurate estimation of the prevalence of prepregnancy health outcomes. Few reliable studies exist that estimate it using demographic health survey data. \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy} uses a novel reweighting method to estimate the nutritional status of women at the time of conception by adjusting the distribution of non-pregnant women to match the observable characteristics of pregnant women in large-scale survey data. This method resulted in a 6.7\% higher pre-pregnancy underweight prevalence when using reweighting methods compared to non-pregnant WRA as a proxy. We follow the same method in this paper to produce more recent estimates using the NFHS-5 and also the first estimates of pre-pregnancy underweight by social group.


\subsection{Data}


Despite the importance of maternal nutrition to maternal and child health outcomes in India, no population-level longitudinal data exists that would allow researchers to observe women before, during, and after pregnancy.  In this study, we estimate prepregnancy underweight by constructing a nonparametric reweighting function that we apply to cross sectional data on the body mass index scores of non-pregnant women.  

This paper uses the most recent data from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS), India's Demographic and Health Survey (DHS). These data are is publicly available from dhsprogram.com. Specifically, we use the fifth round of the NFHS, which was conducted from 2019-2021.  The NFHS-5 is a multi-stage, clustered sample survey that interviewed over 635,000 households and over 720,000 women about a range of topics related to health and family. 

\subsection{Variables}

{\bf{Body mass index.}} Body mass index (BMI) is a person's weight in kilograms divided by her height in meters squared.  The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies someone as ``underweight'' if her body mass index is less than 18.5.  Weight and/or height measurements are missing from 2.8\% of pregnant women and 3.4\% of non-pregnant women; these observations are dropped from the analysis.

{\bf{Pregnancy.}}  The NFHS asks all women ages 15-49 whether they are currently pregnant, and, if so, how many months pregnant they are.  We code a woman as pregnant if she reports being three or more months pregnant.\footnote{If the question on months of pregnancy is not answered, we use reported time since last menstrual period as a measure of gestational duration among women who report that they are pregnant at the time of the survey. Months of pregnancy is missing for only 0.06\% of the pregnant sample.}  We drop women who report pregnancies of one or two months duration because they are a select group of women who are aware of early pregnancies and willing to report them.  As table \ref{regression3plusmopreg} in the online appendix shows, these women are more likely to be in the upper half of the wealth distribution relative to women reporting longer durations of pregnancy. Table \ref{gestdur_distribution} in the online appendix shows the distribution of reported months of gestational duration among women pregnant at the time of the survey. A similar proportion of women report being 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8 months pregnant.

{\bf{Social group.}} The NFHS asks respondents whether their caste is among the constitutionally recognized``Scheduled Castes,'' ``Scheduled Tribes,'' or ``Other Backward Classes.''  It also asks respondents' religion (Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, and others).  Following the India Human Development Survey (IHDS) \citep{IHDS2005}. we combine these variables to define mutually exclusive social groups.  Adivasi and Dalit women are defined as those who report belonging to a Scheduled Tribe or a Scheduled Caste, respectively. Muslim women are defined as those who report their religion as Muslim and do not belong to a Scheduled Caste or Tribe. OBC women are defined as Hindu or Sikh women who report belonging to an Other Backward Class.  Forward Caste women are Hindus who do not belong to a Scheduled Tribe, a Scheduled Caste, or an Other Backward Caste.  About 2.3\% of the women's sample does not fall into one of these categories, e.g. Christians, Jains.  These women are dropped from the analysis for ease of interpretation. Beyond conformity with IHDS social group definitions, we focus on non SC and ST Muslims because Muslims are officially excluded from the Scheduled Caste constitutional provision (and comprise only 2\% of SCs in the sample), but are meaningfully distinct from non-Muslim OBCs. 

{\bf{Predictors of maternal undernutrition and pregnancy.}} Our method for estimating pre-pregnancy underweight, described below, involves matching non-pregnant to pregnant women on a number of variables that predict both pregnancy and body weight:
\begin{itemize}
\item not using modern contraception: a binary indicator for not using pill, IUD, injectables, diaphragm, condom, or male or female sterilization in the last 15 months for non-pregnant women and in the 15 months prior to conception for pregnant women. 
\item less than a primary education: a binary indicator for women self reporting their highest completed grade as "no education" or in the "primary" grades.
\item rural resident: determined by NFHS surveyors. Each district is stratified into urban and rural areas which are then further stratified into primary sampling units. 
\item does not have a body child: an indicator for if women don't report having at least 1 son at home or elsewhere.
\item age category: 15-19, 20-24, 25-29, 30-49
\item parity and birthspacing: combination of parity (0, 1, 2, 3+) with months since the previous live birth (below 2 years, 2-3 years, 3 or more years). For pregnant women, spacing is measured from the previous live birth to the estimated conception date of the index pregnancy (birth date - 9 months). For non-pregnant women, spacing is measured from the previous live birth to the interview date.
\item wealth quartile: quartiles based on wealth index factor score, which is a score generated using principal component analysis of a set of indicators for each household's ownership of owning various goods, ranging from a television to a bicycle or car, source of drinking water, toilet facilities, and flooring materials.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Estimation of pre-pregnancy indicators}

Following \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy}, we apply a nonparametric reweighting procedure to nonpregnant women in the sample so that their distribution of key predictors of pregnancy matches that of pregnant women in the sample. This counterfactual distribution upweights nonpregnant women who are more likely to become pregnant and downweights those less likely, producing estimates that more closely approximate the true values of women's nutritional outcomes at the onset of pregnancy in India. This method addresses the challenge that the NFHS doesn't collect longitudinal data, making it impossible to directly observe prepregnancy outcomes. 


The reweighting procedure was conducted within each social group for estimation of prepregnancy outcomes by social groups. First, all women in the sample were assigned to bins defined by the intersection of indicators that are important determinants of the likelihood of women becoming pregnant in India and are plausibly correlated with body mass [CITE]. 

These indicators include women's use of modern contraception, having either less than two years, between 2 and 3 years, or 3 or more years since their last birth, and being of the age groups 15-19, 20-24, 25-29, or 30-49 (defined for pregnant women based on what is true in the month of conception and for nonpregnant women based on what is true when they are surveyed). The remaining indicators are based on women's having below secondary education, living in rural residence, having one or more living sons, and belonging to each wealth quartile, which are defined for all women based on what is true at the time of the survey. This allows for 2,560 bins, but in the data, the number of bins per social group ranges from 1636-1882. 

Next, the reweighting function \(\psi\) is defined as \(\psi(x)=[f(x|P=1)]/[f(x|P=0)]\), the ratio of the weighted counts of pregnant to nonpregnant women in each bin \(x\).

Finally, the reweighting function is applied as \(\tilde{W}_i = W_i * \psi(x_i)\) where nonpregnant woman \(i\) has original NFHS sampling weight \(\tilde{W}_i\) and bin \(x_i\). By construction, the distribution of \(\tilde{W}_i\) across bins matches that 
of pregnant women, so population averages using these weights provides an estimate of the prepregnancy distribution of maternal nutrition outcomes.

\subsection{Kitagawa(1955)-style decomposition}

India's demographic transition has not occurred uniformly across social groups, reflecting inequalities in socioeconomic development and differences in the pace of fertility decline and birth spacing practices. 

To understand the determinants of differences in prepregnancy underweight across social groups, we conduct a decomposition into the component of the difference due to composition across categories and the component due to differences in within category means, following \cite{kitagawa1955components}. Specifically, we conduct two decompositions. The first is over a 10 category parity variable, where parities above 1 are divided into three subgroups based on whether the woman's last birth was less than 2 years ago, between 2 and 3 years ago, or above 3 years ago. The second is over wealth quartiles. 

Following \cite{kitagawa1955components}, we decompose differences in prepregnancy underweight as

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kitagawa}
\Delta \equiv \bar Y^{f} - \bar Y^{g}
= \Delta_{explained} + \Delta_{unexplained}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{explained} = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \big(p_j^{f} - p_j^{g}\big)\,\frac{Y_j^{f} + Y_j^{g}}{2}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{unexplained} = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \big(Y_j^{f} - Y_j^{g}\big)\,\frac{p_j^{f} + p_j^{g}}{2}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item where \(i=1,...,J\) indexes categories of the decomposition variable (e.g. parity/spacing, for which J=10)
    \item \( \bar Y^f\) and \(\bar Y^g\) are the overall means for groups \(f\) (forward caste) and \(g\) (comparison group)
    \item \(Y_i^f\) and \(Y_i^g\) are the means within category i of groups \(f\) and \(g\) respectively
    \item \(p_i^f\) and \(p_i^g\) are the proportions of women in category \(i\)
    
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}

\subsection*{Sample means of pregnancy covariates (Table \ref{sumstats})}

Table \ref{sumstats} shows sample means of the covariates of pregnancy later used in the reweighting procedure, illustrating two sets of contrasts.

First, pregnant and nonpregnant women have systematically different characteristics. Pregnant women are much younger and at lower parities - 43\% are aged 20–24 (versus 16\% of nonpregnant women), and 42\% have no prior births (versus 30\%). In contrast, nonpregnant women in the sample are much older and at higher parity: 25\% have had 3 or more births with over 3 years of birth spacing from their last birth, compared to only 4\% of pregnant women. This is likely to be the end of childbearing for most women, as total fertility rates are close to 2 for all social groups as seen in table \ref{tfr}. The differences of these means across pregnancy status highlights the the potential biases that come with estimating prepregnancy outcomes simply based on nonpregnant women. This motivates our inclusion of these covariates in the reweighting procedure to alter the distribution of nonpregnant women to match that of pregnant women in our sample.

Second, there are stark differences across social groups, highlighting inequality that reflects in comparisons of prepregnancy nutrition. Nearly half of Adivasi nonpregnant women have less than primary education, compared to only 21\% of forwards caste nonpregnant women. Women in the the lowest wealth quartile makes up nearly half of Adivasi and 27\% of Dalit nonpregnant women, compared to only 10\% of forwards caste nonpregnant women. These patterns are also consistent for pregnant women.


\subsection*{Regressions predicting pregnancy (Table \ref{regression})}

The covariates used in the reweighting procedure are characteristics that demography research has linked to fertility and are also plausibly correlated with body mass [28-34 in \referen{coffey2015prepregnancy}]. To test this, we regress an indicator of pregnancy on these covariates using the full sample of pregnant and nonpregnant women and observe if they are predictive of pregnancy risk. Table \ref{regression} reports results from these linear probability regressions conducted separately for each social group in our analysis. Almost all coefficients are significant in predicting pregnancy, highlighting their importance in being used in reweighting.

For instance, women that didn't use modern contraception when they were most recently nonpregnant were up to 6.5\% more likely to be pregnant across all social groups. Also, women who did not yet have a son were up to 2.6\% more likely to be pregnant across all social groups. Considering parity and birth spacing simultaneously is also important - women who had 1 birth between 2 and 3 years ago were 10.4\% more likely to be pregnant for all social groups combined.

The less predictive, but still statistically significant covariates of pregnancy were wealth quartile, less than primary education, and rural resident. We retain wealth so that we can get accurate pre-pregnancy underweight estimates by wealth quartile. However, we consider reweighting specifications without rural residence and less and primary education, and with finer age bins in Table \ref{specifications}. We find that prepregnancy underweight estimates are relatively stable across these specifications, confirming the validity of our method.

\subsection*{Prepregnancy underweight by social group (Figure \ref{fig:mainresult})}

Figure \ref{fig:mainresult} shows the estimates of prepregnancy underweight by social group. Confidence intervals don't overlap for any two social groups besides forward caste and Muslim, confirming statistically significant social inequalities in prepregnancy underweight at the 95\% level. The burden of prepregnancy undernutrition is highest among Adivasi women: 28\% are underweight at the onset of pregnancy. Dalit and OBC prepregnant women are also at higher risk, with 25\% and 23\% underweight, respectively. On the other hand, prepregnancy underweight is lowest among the forward caste (18\%) and Muslim (17\%) subgroups. Though prepregnancy underweight is lower among Muslim women than forward caste women, this difference is not statistically significant. Across all five groups, 22\% of prepregnant women are underweight, suggesting a relatively larger representation of Adivasi, Dalit, and OBC women among India's prepregnant population. 

\subsection*{Prepregnancy underweight by parity, birth spacing, and wealth quartile (Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel})}

Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} shows distributions of pregnant women and prepregnancy underweight estimates by parity, birth spacing, and wealth quartile categories. These categories are central to understanding social group disparities in prepregnancy health - repeated childbearing with limited recovery time may lead to maternal depletion, and poverty constrains access to adequate nutrition and healthcare.

\subsubsection*{Parity}

Among currently pregnant women, those who are forward caste were most likely among all social groups to be on their first or second birth - almost half are at parity 1, compared to 38-39\% of Muslim and Adivasi pregnant women (Panel A). On the other hand, Muslim pregnant women are the most likely to be at higher parities, with 16\% at parity 4 or higher compared to only 5\% among forward caste and 8\% of OBC pregnant women. This underscores how fertility patterns differ by social group, and is reflected in differences of total fertility rates in table \ref{tfr}.

Prepregnancy underweight is highest among women who have no prior births at 26\%, and drops steeply at parity 2 and 3, where in both cases 19\% of prepregnant women are underweight (Panel B). At parities of 4 or higher, 21\% are underweight.

\subsubsection*{Birth spacing}

Low birth spacing (below 2 years) was most common in the Dalit subgroup of currently pregnant women (28\%) compared to around 19\% among the Adivasi and Muslim subgroups (Panel C). Healthy birth spacing (above 3 years) was most common in the Muslim and forward caste subgroup of currently pregnant women at around 55\%. Prepregnancy underweight is highest among women who have had 2-3 years since their most recent birth (25\%) compared to less than 2 years (23\%) and above 3 years (21\%).

\subsubsection*{Wealth}

Wealth distributions reveal stark inequality (Panel E). Half of Adivasi pregnant women fall in the lowest wealth quartile, compared to only 12\% of forward caste pregnant women. By contrast, nearly 44\% of forward caste women are in the top quartile, compared with fewer than one in five Dalit or Adivasi women. Prepregnancy underweight decreases steadily with wealth, from roughly 30\% in the poorest quartile to 13\% in the richest (declining about 5 percentage points per quartile).

\subsection*{Decompositions of social group differences (Table 3)}

Table \ref{decomposition} shows decompositions of the difference between underweight among forward caste prepregnant women and prepregnant women from other social groups.

Panel A shows that a combined parity and birthspacing variable explains very little of the forward caste advantage in prepregnancy nutrition compared to Adivasi, Dalit, and OBC prepregnant women (-2\% to 4\%). Although a majority of the gap between Muslim and Forward caste prepregnancy underweight is explained by the combined parity and birthspacing variable, the gap is statistically insignificant to begin with.

On the other hand, wealth quartile explains a large portion of the forward caste advantage in prepregnancy underweight relative to the Adivasi, Dalit, and OBC subgroups - between 49\% and 69\%. On the other hand, the small and statistically insignificant difference between forward caste and Muslim prepregnancy underweight becomes 185\% larger when standardizing for wealth quartile. These results are all consistent with the economic advantage of forward caste Indians and the positive relationship between wealth and health.

\section{Discussion}

This study documents large caste based inequalities in prepregnancy underweight in India. At least one in four Dalit or Adivasi women in India are underweight at the onset of pregnancy. Meanwhile, the prevalence of prepregnancy underweight among forward caste and OBC or upper caste Muslim women is up to 11\% lower than other subgroups. 

India as a whole continues to have worse prepregnancy health outcomes relative to countries with less national wealth. We find that the national prevalence of prepregnancy underweight was 22\% in 2019-2021. Although this marks a huge improvement from 42.5\% in 2004-2005, it's still considerably higher than the comparable figure in Sub-Saharan African countries from 2000-2010 of 16.5\% \cife{coffey2015prepregnancy}.

Prepregnancy underweight is an important risk factor for adverse birth outcomes. However, no studies show the prevalence of adverse birth outcomes disaggregated by social group in India. Thus we can use back of the envelope calculations to apply risk ratios from other studies to the findings of our paper to estimate the proportion of preterm births attributable to prepregnancy underweight. 



\begin{equation}
    ABO = \frac{p \times(RR-1)}{1+ p\times(RR-1)}    
\end{equation}

where $ABO$ is rate of an adverse birth outcome, $p$ is proportion of prepregnancy underweight, and $RR$ is the risk ratio. 

For instance, studies from developed countries find adjusted risk ratios of prepregnancy underweight for pre-term birth and low birthweight between 1.2 and 1.7 \cite{girsen2016women} \cite{hur2009512} \cite{wenman2004prospective} \cite{diamond2024impact}.
Considering an upper-bound risk ratio of 1.7, we estimate that underweight accounts for about 16\% of preterm births among Adivasi women compared to 11\% among forward caste women. With a baseline PTB rate of 13\% nationally \cite{jana2023correlates}, this translates to roughly 7 additional preterm births per 1,000 deliveries attributable to underweight for Adivasi women relative to forward caste.

\subsection*{Additional prepregnancy outcomes by social group}

\subsubsection{Weight gain method 1}

The first method of estimating weight gain starts by estimating monthly weight gain of pregnant women at 3+ months gestational duration as $\hat{\beta}$ from an OLS regression of the form
\begin{equation}
    weight_i = \alpha + \beta month_i + \tau C_i + \epsilon_i
\end{equation}

where $weight_i$ is the weight in kilograms of pregnant woman $i$. $month_i$ is month of gestation, calculated as described in the methods section, and $C_i$ are controls, including women's current ages (single year), years of education, number of living children, urban or rural residence, wealth quintile, as well as state-month fixed effects.

Assuming women gain 10 times more weight in their second and third trimesters than in their first, we estimate weight gain for a full-term pregnancy as 
\begin{equation}
    \hat{weight gain} = 1.1\times6\times\hat{\beta}
\end{equation}

In table A1, the 95\% confidence intervals for weight gain method 1 are calculated as $1.1\times6\times\hat{\beta} \pm 1.96\times se_{\hat{\beta}}$.

\subsubsection{Weight gain method 2}

The second method of estimating weight gain takes the difference of the average weight of pregnant women at gestational duration of 9 months or more and the average prepregnancy weight using the nonparametric reweighting method. 


\begin{equation}
    \hat{weight gain} = (\bar{W}_9-\bar{W}_0)+(0.5)\hat{\beta}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{W}_9$ is the average weight of pregnant women at gestational duration of 9 months or more, and $\bar{W}_0$ is the average prepregnancy weight using the nonparametric reweighting method. To account for additional weight gain between the time of survey and the end of pregnancy for women of gestational duration of 9 months or more, we assume these women are at the midpoint of their last month of pregnancy and add the accordingly scaled $\hat{\beta}$ from the regression in weight gain method 1.

This offers a benchmark to method 1, which may be inaccurate if the assumption is false that women gain 10 times more weight in their second and third trimesters than in their first, or if gestational durations are incorrectly reported.
