\title{Fertility and Maternal Nutrition by Indian Social Group}
\author{authors}
\date{\today}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Maternal undernutrition is an important cause of child mortality and morbidity. Prior research finds that maternal undernutrition, measured by prepregnancy underweight, is common in India. This paper is the first to document differences in maternal undernutrition by Indian social group. We apply reweighting decomposition methods to National Family Health Survey, 2019-21 (NFHS-5) data to document that prepregnancy underweight is about 70\% higher among Adivasis and 40\% higher among Dalits than among Forward Caste Hindus and Muslims. Although high fertility and low birth spacing are known to contribute to maternal undernutrition in other contexts, we find that these factors do not explain social group differences in maternal undernutrition. Instead, accounting for differences in economic status explains much of the prepregnancy underweight gaps between Adivasis and Forward Castes, and between Dalits and Forward Castes. Accounting for wealth differences reveals that despite multidimensional disadvantage, Muslims have lower rates of prepregnancy underweight than Forward Caste Hindus.

\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Prepregnancy undernutrition is an important determinant of maternal mortality, women's long term wellbeing, and intergenerational population health outcomes. In undernourished women, limited fat, muscle, and micronutrient reserves—combined with reduced immune function and blood volume—make it difficult to meet the additional energy demands of pregnancy, increasing the risk of complications for both mother and child. Evidence suggests that fetal growth is most vulnerable to undernutrition "during the peri-implantation period and the period of rapid placental development", a window that often precedes pregnancy recognition among Indian women. \cite{bazer2004maternal}

India is a unique case with disproportionately poor prepregnancy health outcomes. \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy} finds that 42.2\% of prepregnant women in India are underweight compared with 16.5\% of prepregnant women in sub-Saharan Africa, and weight gain during pregnancy does not compensate. Additionally, \cite{miller2025preconception} finds that India shows the highest rates of "pre-conception" underweight in South Asia. This can be linked to India's poor child health outcomes. \cite{ahmed2013global} finds that in South Asia, maternal malnutrition alone is responsible for 25–50\% of intrauterine growth restriction. (I can't find the proper source of this, but it's widely cited). 

Despite the urgency, India has no national pregnancy monitoring system for accurate estimation of the national prevalence of prepregnancy health outcomes. Few reliable studies exist that estimate it using demographic health survey data. \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy} uses a novel reweighting method to estimate the nutritional status of women at the time of conception by adjusting the distribution of non-pregnant women to match the observable characteristics of pregnant women in large-scale survey data. This method resulted in a 6.7\% higher pre-pregnancy underweight prevalence when using reweighting methods compared to non-pregnant WRA as a proxy. This paper follows the same method to produce more recent estimates using the NFHS-5 and also the first estimates of pre-pregnancy underweight by social group.

Disaggregation by social group is important because disparities in maternal health and nutrition across caste and religion are well documented, and national averages mask stark inequalities. \cite{borooah2010inequality} shows that, even after controlling for socioeconomic and demographic factors, life expectancy among Scheduled Tribes (STs) was 5 years lower, Scheduled Castes (SCs) 7 years lower, and OBC Muslims 8.6 years lower than among upper-caste Hindus (M\&HC Survey 2004). \cite{ramaiah2015health} the under-five mortality rate (U5MR) was 14 deaths higher among Scheduled Castes (88 vs. 74 nationally), while infant mortality among SCs and STs was 13.1 and 12.3 deaths higher, respectively, compared to the general category (NFHS-3). \cite{let2024prevalence}: the prevalence of anemia among women of reproductive age was 66.9\% among Adivasi women compared to a national average of 54\%.


We find that 26.5\% of Adivasi women and 22.3\% of Dalit women were underweight before pregnancy, compared to 15.5\% of forward-caste Hindus and Muslims. This represents a 70\% higher rate among Adivasis and a 43\% higher rate among Dalits. 

This paper also contributes to a growing literature on the determinants of social group disparities in health. A prominent explanation for poor maternal health among marginalized women is the maternal depletion hypothesis, which posits that repeated, closely spaced pregnancies lead to cumulative physiological stress and undernutrition. Indeed, preconception risk, low birth spacing, and high parity is higher among women of marginalized communities and of low socioeconomic status.

\cite{singh2024wealth}: using NFHS-5, odds of short birth interval relative to general caste - Scheduled Caste (1.18), Scheduled Tribes (1.14), and Other Backward Classes (1.12). Women whose last child had passed away were also significantly more likely to have SBI (2.35). SBI prevalence was higher among poorer mothers (Richest: 33.8\% vs. Poorest: 52.9\%) and mothers from communities with lower average schooling levels (1.18).


\cite{kuppusamy2023high}: women with no formal education and in the poorest wealth quintile were much more likely to have high risk pregnancy (NFHS-5)

\cite{doke2022prevalence} found that among women desiring pregnancy in Nashik, selected risk factors were significantly more prevalent in tribal areas, where 7.7\% of high-parity women (4 or more births) still wished to conceive, compared to 3\% in non-tribal areas.

\vspace{1.5em}

Studies have shown associations between short birth intervals, high parity, and adverse maternal outcomes.

\cite{kundu2025short}: short birth spacing increased the likelihood of maternal undernutrition (1.39 times more prevalent), incomplete ANC, low birth weight, infant and child mortality (NFHS-5).

\cite{bauserman2020relationship}: short birth intervals had increased risk of neonatal death, stillbirth, and low birth weight (NICHD of DRC, Zambia, Kenya, Guatemala, India, and Pakistan)

\cite{dhingra2021effects}: birth spacing length explains the firstborn height advantage


\vspace{1.5em}

However, the extent to which maternal depletion explains social group disparities in health remains an open empirical question. We find that \textbf{differences in parity and birth spacing account for only up to 5\% of the gap} (between forward caste and Adivasi or Dalit or OBC women) despite forwards caste women being more likely to be on their first birth or have 3+ years of birth spacing. This implies that other structural factors—such as poverty, discrimination, or access to health services—may play a larger role in driving these disparities.

\vspace{1.5em}

To further investigate these structural factors, we conduct a decomposition by wealth status. Since caste and class are deeply intertwined in India, understanding how much of the caste-based gap in pre-pregnancy undernutrition is mediated by economic inequality can shed light on the relative importance of poverty versus social exclusion.

Indeed, wealth accounts for about 60\% of the gap between Dalit and forward-caste women and Adivasi and forward-caste women, and  53.3\% of the gap between OBC and forward-caste women.

\vspace{1.5em}
\textbf{Although deprivation can explain a majority of the gap, up to 40\%, a very large component, remains unexplained.} This adds to a growing literature on the unobservable effects of caste on health in India.

[\cite{geruso2014sanitation}, \cite{gupta2020caste}, \cite{lopalo2019consequences}]


\cite{bora2019persistent}: 83\% of the caste-based gap in the under-five deaths
is due to the distribution of women’s level of educational attainment and household wealth
between the SC/ST and non-SC/ST population (NFHS).

\cite{dutta2020effect}: Caste had an independent effect on anaemia and sickness absenteeism in school children, unexplained by inter-caste economic disparities (data collected in Odisha).

\cite{van2009decomposing}: Differences in the distribution of the determinants were responsible for about 65\% of the gap between the ST/SC and the remaining population in average height-for-age z score




\vspace{1.5em}

The gap between pre-pregnancy underweight of Muslim women that weren't SC or ST and forwards caste women was not statistically significant. 

Despite multidimensional disadvantage, Muslims have lower rates of pre-pregnancy underweight than forward caste Hindus, and this gap widens after accounting for wealth differences.

\subsection{Data}

This paper uses data from the DHS, which are publicly available from dhsprogram.com. Specifically, we use the fifth round of India's National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5) conducted from 2019-2021. BMI measurements, from which all outcomes in this paper are constructed, are missing from 2.8\% of pregnant women and 3.4\% of non-pregnant women; these observations are dropped from the analysis. Women's sampling weights are used for computation of all summary statistics.

We define five mutually exclusive social groups for which we restrict the sample for analysis: Adivasi, Dalit, OBC (Other Backward Class), forward caste, and Muslim. Adivasi and Dalit women are those who report belonging to a Scheduled Tribe or Scheduled Caste, respectively. Muslim women are those who report being Muslim and not belonging to a Scheduled Caste or Tribe. OBC women are Hindu or Sikh women who report belonging to an Other Backward Class. Thus, we restrict the sample for analysis to women who are Scheduled Tribe, Scheduled Caste, Muslim, both OBC and Sikh, and Hindu. \subsection{Estimation of pre-pregnancy indicators}

Following \cite{coffey2015prepregnancy}, we apply a nonparametric reweighting procedure to nonpregnant women in the sample so that their distribution of key predictors of pregnancy matches that of pregnant women in the sample. This counterfactual distribution upweights nonpregnant women who are more likely to become pregnant and downweights those less likely, producing estimates that more closely approximate the true values of women's nutritional outcomes at the onset of pregnancy in India. This method addresses the challenge that the NFHS doesn't collect longitudinal data, making it impossible to directly observe prepregnancy outcomes. 


The reweighting procedure was conducted within each social group for estimation of prepregnancy outcomes by social groups. First, all women in the sample were assigned to bins defined by the intersection of indicators that are important determinants of the likelihood of women becoming pregnant in India and are plausibly correlated with body mass [CITE]. 

These indicators include women's use of modern contraception, having either less than two years, between 2 and 3 years, or 3 or more years since their last birth, and being of the age groups 15-19, 20-24, 25-29, or 30-49 (defined for pregnant women based on what is true in the month of conception and for nonpregnant women based on what is true when they are surveyed). The remaining indicators are based on women's having below secondary education, living in rural residence, having one or more living sons, and belonging to each wealth quartile, which are defined for all women based on what is true at the time of the survey. This allows for 2,560 bins, but in the data, the number of bins per social group ranges from 1636-1882. 

Next, the reweighting function \(\psi\) is defined as \(\psi(x)=[f(x|P=1)]/[f(x|P=0)]\), the ratio of the weighted counts of pregnant to nonpregnant women in each bin \(x\).

Finally, the reweighting function is applied as \(\tilde{W}_i = W_i * \psi(x_i)\) where nonpregnant woman \(i\) has original NFHS sampling weight \(\tilde{W}_i\) and bin \(x_i\). By construction, the distribution of \(\tilde{W}_i\) across bins matches that 
of pregnant women, so population averages using these weights provides an estimate of the prepregnancy distribution of maternal nutrition outcomes.

\subsection{Kitagawa(1955)-style decomposition}

India's demographic transition has not occurred uniformly across social groups, reflecting inequalities in socioeconomic development and differences in the pace of fertility decline and birth spacing practices. 

To understand the determinants of differences in prepregnancy underweight across social groups, we conduct a decomposition into the component of the difference due to composition across categories and the component due to differences in within category means, following \cite{kitagawa1955components}. Specifically, we conduct two decompositions. The first is over a 10 category parity variable, where parities above 1 are divided into three subgroups based on whether the woman's last birth was less than 2 years ago, between 2 and 3 years ago, or above 3 years ago. The second is over wealth quartiles. 

Following \cite{kitagawa1955components}, we decompose differences in prepregnancy underweight as

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kitagawa}
\Delta \equiv \bar Y^{f} - \bar Y^{g}
= \Delta_{explained} + \Delta_{unexplained}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{explained} = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \big(p_j^{f} - p_j^{g}\big)\,\frac{Y_j^{f} + Y_j^{g}}{2}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{unexplained} = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \big(Y_j^{f} - Y_j^{g}\big)\,\frac{p_j^{f} + p_j^{g}}{2}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item where \(i=1,...,J\) indexes categories of the decomposition variable (e.g. parity/spacing, for which J=10)
    \item \( \bar Y^f\) and \(\bar Y^g\) are the overall means for groups \(f\) (forward caste) and \(g\) (comparison group)
    \item \(Y_i^f\) and \(Y_i^g\) are the means within category i of groups \(f\) and \(g\) respectively
    \item \(p_i^f\) and \(p_i^g\) are the proportions of women in category \(i\)
    
\end{itemize}










\section{Results}

\subsection*{Sample means of pregnancy covariates (Table \ref{sumstats})}

Table \ref{sumstats} shows sample means of the covariates later used in the reweighting procedure by social group and pregnancy status. This highlights the systematic differences between pregnant and nonpregnant women in the sample, and the potential biases that come with estimating prepregnancy outcomes simply based on the distribution of nonpregnant women. 

Pregnant women are younger - 43\% are in the 20-24 age group compared to only 16\% of nonpregnant women. Pregnant women are also more likely to be at lower parities - 42\% have no prior births compared to only 30\% of nonpregnant women. Also, 16\% of pregnant women have had 1 birth over 3 years ago, compared to only 8\% of nonpregnant women. 

On the other hand, nonpregnant women in the sample are more likely to be later in their childbearing careers. 25\% have had 3 or more births with over 3 years of birth spacing from their last birth, compared to only 4\% of pregnant women. These patterns are consistent across social groups. These differences motivate our inclusion of these covariates in the reweighting procedure to alter the distribution of nonpregnant women to match that of pregnant women in our sample.

Differences across social groups are also evident, highlighting inequality that reflects in comparisons of prepregnancy nutrition. Among nonpregnant women, 48\% of Adivasi and 41\% of Dalit women have less than primary education, compared to only 21\% of forwards caste women. Women in the the lowest wealth quartile makes up 49\% of Adivasi and 27\% of Dalit nonpregnant women, compared to only 10\% of forwards caste nonpregnant women. These patterns are consistent for pregnant women as well.


\subsection*{Regressions predicting pregnancy (Table \ref{regression})}

The covariates used in the reweighting procedure are characteristics of pregnant women that when observed in a nonpregnant women, may indicate her likelihood of soon becoming pregnant. To test this assumption, we regress an indicator of pregnancy on these covariates using the full sample of pregnant and nonpregnant women and observe the size and significance of the coefficients.

Table \ref{regression} reports results from these regressions, conducted separately for each social group in our analysis. 

\subsection*{Prepregnancy underweight by social group (Figure \ref{fig:mainresult})}

Figure \ref{fig:mainresult} shows the estimates of prepregnancy underweight by social group. Confidence intervals don't overlap for any two social groups besides forward caste and Muslim, confirming statistically significant social inequalities in prepregnancy underweight at the 95\% level. The burden of prepregnancy undernutrition is highest among Adivasi women: 28\% are underweight at the onset of pregnancy. Dalit and OBC prepregnant women are also at higher risk, with 25\% and 23\% underweight, respectively. On the other hand, prepregnancy underweight is lowest among the forward caste (18\%) and Muslim (17\%) subgroups. Though prepregnancy underweight is lower among Muslim women than forward caste women, this difference is not statistically significant. Across all five groups, 22\% of prepregnant women are underweight, suggesting a relatively larger representation of Adivasi, Dalit, and OBC women among India's prepregnant population. 

\subsection*{Prepregnancy underweight by parity, birth spacing, and wealth quartile (Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel})}

Figure \ref{fig:sixpanel} shows distributions of pregnant women and prepregnancy underweight estimates by parity, birth spacing, and wealth quartile categories. 

\subsubsection*{Parity}

Among currently pregnant women, those who are forward caste were most likely among all social groups to be on their first or second birth - almost half are at parity 1, compared to 37.8\% of Muslim women and 39.2\% of Adivasi pregnant women (Panel A). On the other hand, Muslim pregnant women are the most likely to be at higher parities, with 16.3\% at parity 4 or higher compared to only 5.2\% among forward caste pregnant women and 8.4\% among OBC pregnant women.

Prepregnancy underweight is highest among women who have no prior births at 26\%, and drops steeply at parity 2 and 3, where in both cases 19\% of prepregnant women are underweight (Panel B). At parities of 4 or higher, 21\% are underweight.

\subsubsection*{Birth spacing}

Low birth spacing (below 2 years) was most common in the Dalit subgroup of currently pregnant women (28\%) compared to 19.9\% and 19.4\% among the Adivasi and Muslim subgroup, respectively (Panel C). Healthy birth spacing (above 3 years) was most common in the Muslim and forward caste subgroup of currently pregnant women at 55.3\% and 54.2\%, respectively. Prepregnancy underweight is highest among women who have had 2-3 years since their most recent birth (25\%) compared to less than 2 years (23\%) and above 3 years (21\%).

\subsubsection*{Wealth}

Wealth distributions reveal stark inequality (Panel E). Half of Adivasi pregnant women fall in the lowest wealth quartile, compared to only 12\% of forward caste women. By contrast, nearly 44\% of forward caste women are in the top quartile, compared with fewer than one in five Dalit or Adivasi women. Prepregnancy underweight decreases steadily with wealth, from roughly 30\% in the poorest quartile to 13\% in the richest (a decline of about 5 percentage points per quartile).

Table \ref{decomposition} shows decompositions of the difference between underweight among forward caste prepregnant women and prepregnant women from other social groups.

Panel A shows that a combined parity and birthspacing variable explains very little of the forward caste advantage in prepregnancy nutrition compared to Adivasi, Dalit, and OBC prepregnant women (-1.81\%-3.95\%).

Although 63\% of the gap between Muslim and Forward caste prepregnancy underweight is explained by the combined parity and birthspacing variable, the gap is statistically insignificant to begin with.

On the other hand, wealth quartile explains a large portion of the forward caste advantage in prepregnancy underweight relative to the Adivasi, Dalit, and OBC subgroups.

\subsection*{Additional prepregnancy outcomes by social group}

\subsubsection{Weight gain method 1}

The first method of estimating weight gain starts by estimating monthly weight gain of pregnant women at 3+ months gestational duration as $\hat{\beta}$ from an OLS regression of the form
\begin{equation}
    weight_i = \alpha + \beta month_i + \tau C_i + \epsilon_i
\end{equation}

where $weight_i$ is the weight in kilograms of pregnant woman $i$. $month_i$ is month of gestation, calculated as described in the methods section, and $C_i$ are controls, including women's current ages (single year), years of education, number of living children, urban or rural residence, wealth quintile, as well as state-month fixed effects.

Assuming women gain 10 times more weight in their second and third trimesters than in their first, we estimate weight gain for a full-term pregnancy as 
\begin{equation}
    \hat{weight gain} = 1.1\times6\times\hat{\beta}
\end{equation}

In table A1, the 95\% confidence intervals for weight gain method 1 are calculated as $1.1\times6\times\hat{\beta} \pm 1.96\times se_{\hat{\beta}}$.

\subsubsection{Weight gain method 2}

The second method of estimating weight gain takes the difference of the average weight of pregnant women at gestational duration of 9 months or more and the average prepregnancy weight using the nonparametric reweighting method. 


\begin{equation}
    \hat{weight gain} = (\bar{W}_9-\bar{W}_0)+(0.5)\hat{\beta}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{W}_9$ is the average weight of pregnant women at gestational duration of 9 months or more, and $\bar{W}_0$ is the average prepregnancy weight using the nonparametric reweighting method. To account for additional weight gain between the time of survey and the end of pregnancy for women of gestational duration of 9 months or more, we assume these women are at the midpoint of their last month of pregnancy and add the accordingly scaled $\hat{\beta}$ from the regression in weight gain method 1.

This offers a benchmark to method 1, which may be inaccurate if the assumption is false that women gain 10 times more weight in their second and third trimesters than in their first, or if gestational durations are incorrectly reported.
